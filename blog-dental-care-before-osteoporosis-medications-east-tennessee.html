Why Dental Care Matters Before Osteoporosis Medication

A dental checkup can help reduce avoidable complications before certain bone-strengthening medicines begin.

Published September 25, 2026  |  Elite Dental

If your physician plans to start a medicine for osteoporosis or another bone condition, you may be asked to visit your dentist first. Common examples include alendronate (Fosamax), risedronate (Actonel), zoledronic acid (Reclast), denosumab (Prolia), and similar medications. These treatments can play an important role in preventing fractures. A dental exam does not replace that medical care or mean the medicine is unsafe. It helps your medical and dental teams plan ahead.

The main concern is a rare condition called medication-related osteonecrosis of the jaw, often shortened to MRONJ. In this condition, an area of jawbone does not heal normally after it is exposed. Risk varies greatly based on the medicine, dose, length of treatment, medical history, and whether the drug is being used for osteoporosis or at higher doses as part of cancer care.

How Big Is the Risk?

For most patients taking standard osteoporosis doses, the risk of MRONJ is low. It is generally higher for patients receiving stronger or more frequent intravenous or injectable treatment for cancer involving the bones. Other factors can raise risk, including smoking, poorly controlled diabetes, gum disease, ill-fitting dentures, steroid use, and a history of invasive dental procedures during therapy.

The goal is not to frighten patients away from treatment. Osteoporosis can cause serious fractures and disability. Your physician is weighing those risks when recommending medication. Our job as your dental team is to reduce preventable oral problems and communicate with your physician when treatment planning overlaps.

What Happens at the Dental Visit?

We examine your teeth, gums, existing dental work, and any areas that may be infected. Dental X-rays may be recommended based on your history and symptoms. We also review your medication list, the exact bone medicine being considered, why it was prescribed, and when your physician wants treatment to begin.

If your mouth is healthy, you may only need routine preventive care and a plan for follow-up. If we find an abscess, severe gum infection, a non-restorable tooth, or another urgent problem, it may be better to address it before the medication starts. This can reduce the chance that an extraction or other invasive procedure becomes necessary later.

Bring These Details

Tell us the medication name, dose, reason it was prescribed, planned start date, and prescribing clinician. If you have already begun treatment, include the date of your first dose. Accurate details help us assess your individual risk instead of making assumptions.

Do You Need to Delay Medication?

Not automatically. Your dentist should not independently tell you to delay or stop an osteoporosis medicine, and you should never pause a dose on your own. The need for dental treatment, the urgency of your medical care, and your overall risk must be considered together. When needed, Elite Dental can coordinate with your prescribing physician so everyone is working from the same information.

A so-called drug holiday is not appropriate for every patient and may not remove the dental risk. Some medicines remain in bone for years, while stopping others can create a rapid increase in fracture risk. Any change must come from the clinician managing your bone health.

Protecting Your Mouth During Treatment

Good daily care becomes especially valuable once treatment begins. Brush twice a day with fluoride toothpaste, clean between your teeth daily, and keep regular dental visits. Report loose teeth, swelling, drainage, exposed bone, a sore that does not heal, or persistent jaw pain promptly. Most mouth discomfort is not MRONJ, but ongoing symptoms deserve an examination.

Denture wearers should also watch for rubbing or sore spots. A denture that once fit well can become unstable as the mouth changes. Adjusting it early may prevent repeated irritation. If you smoke, quitting improves healing and lowers several oral health risks beyond this specific condition.

What If You Need an Extraction Later?

Sometimes a tooth must be removed even after bone medication has started. That decision is made individually. We consider whether the tooth can predictably be saved, the danger of leaving infection untreated, your medication exposure, and your medical risk factors. We may consult your physician or refer you to an oral surgeon when appropriate.

The best strategy is prevention. Treating decay early, controlling gum disease, and repairing damaged teeth before they become emergencies can help you avoid more invasive care. If your physician has recommended osteoporosis treatment, schedule your dental evaluation promptly so necessary care does not create an avoidable delay.
