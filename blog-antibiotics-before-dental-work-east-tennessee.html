Do I Need Antibiotics Before Dental Work? Heart Conditions, Joint Replacements, and Premedication

Years ago, almost anyone with a heart murmur or a new hip took a pill before a cleaning. The rules have changed. Here is where they stand now.

Published November 4, 2026  |  Elite Dental

"Do I need to take my antibiotic before this appointment?" We hear it every week, and it is a fair question. Many patients were told 10 or 20 years ago to take antibiotics before every dental visit, and nobody ever told them the advice changed. Others have a new joint replacement or a heart procedure and are not sure what applies to them. This guide explains the current recommendations in plain language so you know what to ask.

The Short Version

Still recommended: artificial heart valves, a past heart infection (endocarditis), certain congenital heart conditions, and some heart transplant patients.
No longer routinely recommended: mitral valve prolapse, most heart murmurs, stents, bypass surgery, pacemakers, and most hip and knee replacements.
Gray areas: a weakened immune system or a surgeon with a specific concern. We coordinate with your physician.
Always: tell us about any heart or joint surgery, even if it seems unrelated.

Why Antibiotics Before Dental Work Was a Thing

Your mouth naturally has a lot of bacteria. During cleanings, extractions, and other procedures that involve the gums, small amounts of those bacteria can enter the bloodstream for a short time. In a healthy person, the immune system clears them quickly. The worry was that in a few people, those bacteria could settle on a damaged heart valve or an artificial joint and start a serious infection.

The answer was a single dose of antibiotic before treatment, called premedication or antibiotic prophylaxis. Over time, researchers found two things. First, everyday activities like chewing and brushing put bacteria into the bloodstream far more often than dental visits ever do. Second, giving antibiotics to large numbers of people carries its own risks, including allergic reactions, stomach problems, C. diff infections, and antibiotic resistance. So the guidelines were narrowed to the people who truly benefit.

Heart Conditions That Still Need Premedication

The American Heart Association and the American Dental Association recommend antibiotics before certain dental procedures for people at the highest risk of a bad outcome from a heart infection. That includes people with:

  • An artificial (prosthetic) heart valve, including valves placed through a catheter
  • Prosthetic material used to repair a heart valve, such as an annuloplasty ring
  • A history of infective endocarditis
  • Certain congenital heart conditions, especially ones that are unrepaired, recently repaired with prosthetic material, or repaired with a remaining defect
  • A heart transplant with a problem in one of the heart valves

For these patients, premedication is recommended before procedures that involve the gums or the area around the root tip, or that break through the lining of the mouth. That covers cleanings, deep cleanings, extractions, and many surgical procedures. It is not needed for things like routine x-rays or a numbing shot through healthy tissue.

Heart Conditions That No Longer Need It

This is where many patients are surprised. Under current guidelines, antibiotics are generally not recommended before dental work for:

  • Mitral valve prolapse
  • Most heart murmurs
  • Rheumatic heart disease without an artificial valve
  • Coronary stents or bypass surgery
  • Pacemakers and implanted defibrillators

If you were told years ago to premedicate for one of these, it is worth asking your cardiologist whether that advice still applies. Good gum health matters for your heart in other ways, too, which we cover in our article on gum disease and heart health.

What About Hip and Knee Replacements?

For most people with a joint replacement, the answer today is no. The American Dental Association reviewed the research and found no clear link between dental procedures and infections in artificial joints. Its guidance says antibiotics are generally not recommended before dental work to prevent joint infection.

There are exceptions. Your orthopedic surgeon may still want you to premedicate if you have a weakened immune system, such as from chemotherapy, high-dose steroids, uncontrolled diabetes, or an autoimmune condition, or if you have had a joint infection before. Some surgeons also prefer premedication for a period after surgery. When a surgeon recommends it, we follow that recommendation and ask them to send the prescription.

The best time to deal with dental problems is before the joint surgery, not after. Our guide to dental clearance before joint replacement explains why many surgeons ask for a dental checkup first.

How Premedication Works If You Need It

If you do need it, it is simple. The usual approach is a single dose of antibiotic, most often amoxicillin, taken 30 to 60 minutes before your appointment. You do not need to take it for days before or after. If you are allergic to penicillin, there are other options, so make sure we and your physician know about the allergy.

A few tips that make it easier:

  • Keep your medical history current. Tell us about any new heart procedure, valve repair, or joint surgery.
  • Bring your list of medications, especially if you take blood thinners.
  • Take the dose before you leave home so you do not have to rush in the parking lot.
  • Forgot your dose? Call us before your visit. We can often give it in the office and wait a short time.

The Best Protection Is a Healthy Mouth

Here is the part that gets lost. Because chewing and brushing put bacteria into your bloodstream every day, the most important thing for anyone with a heart or joint concern is keeping gums healthy and infections under control. Bleeding, inflamed gums let more bacteria through. An untreated dental abscess is a steady source of infection. Regular cleanings, treating gum disease, and fixing problem teeth early protect you far more than any single pill.

How We Can Help

At our Dandridge and Jefferson City offices, we review your medical history at every visit and flag anything that may call for premedication. When a case falls into a gray area, we contact your cardiologist or orthopedic surgeon so everyone is on the same page. If you have been taking antibiotics for years and are not sure you still need them, bring it up at your next visit and we will help you sort it out.

Common Questions

Do I need antibiotics before a dental cleaning if I have a joint replacement?

For most people with a hip or knee replacement, no. Current dental and orthopedic guidance says antibiotics are generally not needed before dental work to protect a joint replacement. If your immune system is weakened or your surgeon has a specific reason, they may still recommend it, so tell us about your joint and we can coordinate with your surgeon.

Do I need antibiotics before dental work for mitral valve prolapse or a heart murmur?

Usually not. Mitral valve prolapse, most heart murmurs, stents, bypass surgery, and pacemakers are no longer on the list for antibiotic premedication. The list now focuses on artificial heart valves, prior endocarditis, certain congenital heart conditions, and some heart transplant patients.

What happens if I forget to take my premedication antibiotic?

Call the office before your visit. Depending on the procedure, we may give the dose in the office and wait, or move your appointment. If the antibiotic is taken late, it can still be given up to two hours after the procedure, but it is best taken 30 to 60 minutes before.
