Cracked or Broken Denture? What to Do Next

A damaged denture can disrupt eating and speaking, but a quick home repair can make the problem worse.

Published August 22, 2026  |  Elite Dental

A denture can crack while you are eating, slip from your hands during cleaning, or gradually weaken after years of daily use. Sometimes the damage is obvious, such as a denture that breaks into two pieces. Other times it begins as a thin line in the acrylic, a loose tooth, or a new spot that flexes when you bite.

Whatever the cause, stop wearing a denture that is sharp, unstable, or visibly cracked. Continuing to chew with it may turn a repairable fracture into a complete break. It can also irritate your gums, change your bite, or create a choking risk if a tooth or small piece comes loose.

What to Do When a Denture Breaks

Remove the denture carefully and collect every piece you can find. Rinse the pieces gently with cool water, place them in a clean container, and call your dental office. If the denture is still in one piece but has a crack, avoid bending it to see how far the crack extends. That test can finish the break.

Keep the denture moist unless your dentist gives you different instructions. Plain water is usually appropriate for short-term storage. Do not use hot or boiling water because heat can warp the acrylic. Bring the denture, any loose teeth, and your storage container to the appointment.

Skip the Household Glue

Super glue, craft glue, and do-it-yourself repair kits can be toxic, rough, or difficult to remove. They may also hold the pieces in the wrong position, making a professional repair less predictable.

Why Dentures Crack

Dropping a denture onto a hard sink or tile floor is a common cause of sudden damage. A denture can also weaken because of normal wear, repeated chewing forces, or changes in the fit. The bone and gum tissue beneath a denture naturally change over time. When the denture no longer fits evenly, pressure may concentrate in one area and stress the acrylic.

Grinding or clenching can add force, especially at night. Chewing ice, hard candy, or very tough foods may contribute as well. In some cases, a previous repair, thin acrylic, or an altered bite creates a weak point. Finding the reason for the fracture matters because repairing the crack without correcting the cause may lead to another break.

Can the Denture Be Repaired?

Many clean fractures and loose denture teeth can be repaired, but the right solution depends on the location of the damage, the age of the denture, and how well it fit before it broke. A dentist needs to examine both the denture and your mouth. The pieces must be aligned accurately so your bite does not change.

A simple laboratory repair may be enough when the denture fit and bite are still acceptable. If the denture has become loose, a reline may be recommended to improve contact with the gums. A denture that is badly worn, repeatedly repaired, severely warped, or no longer matched to your mouth may need replacement instead.

Do Not Ignore a Hairline Crack

A small crack rarely becomes stronger on its own. Moisture, temperature changes, and chewing forces can allow it to spread. Early evaluation may preserve more of the original denture and reduce the chance that it breaks at an inconvenient time. A clicking sound, movement between sections, or a sudden change in fit deserves attention even if you cannot see damage.

Call promptly if the denture causes bleeding, a deep sore, significant swelling, or severe pain. If you swallowed or inhaled a loose denture tooth and are coughing, choking, wheezing, or having trouble breathing, seek emergency medical care immediately.

How to Reduce the Risk of Another Break

Clean your denture over a folded towel or a sink partly filled with water so a dropped denture has a softer landing. Use a denture brush and products intended for dentures. Avoid hot water, abrasive toothpaste, and bending metal clasps. Remove and store the denture as directed, and keep it away from pets that may chew it.

Regular dental visits remain important even if you no longer have natural teeth. Your dentist can check the fit, bite, gum tissue, and oral health. If your denture rocks, pinches, requires increasing adhesive, or feels different when you chew, have it evaluated before a crack develops.

A broken denture is frustrating, but guessing at a repair can cost more time and money. Protect the pieces, avoid glue, and let a dental professional determine whether repair, reline, or replacement offers the safest result. Until your appointment, choose foods you can manage safely without the damaged appliance. Do not force the denture back into place if it catches, rocks, or hurts.
