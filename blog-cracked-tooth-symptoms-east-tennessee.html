Cracked Tooth Symptoms: Warning Signs You Should Not Ignore

A cracked tooth can cause confusing, on-and-off pain. Knowing the early signs may help you save the tooth before the crack grows.

Published September 23, 2026  |  Elite Dental

You bite into something firm and feel a quick, sharp pain. A moment later, it is gone. Maybe cold water suddenly bothers one side of your mouth, but you cannot identify the exact tooth. These are classic ways a cracked tooth can announce itself.

Cracks are not always visible, and symptoms often come and go. That makes them easy to dismiss until the tooth breaks or the nerve becomes inflamed. What Does a Cracked Tooth Feel Like?

The most common symptom is sharp pain while chewing, especially when you release your bite. A crack can allow part of the tooth to flex under pressure. When the pressure is released, the movement may irritate the nerve inside the tooth and create a brief jolt.

You may also notice sensitivity to cold, heat, or sweets. The discomfort might be difficult to reproduce and may seem to move between teeth. Some patients can eat normally for days, then feel pain when biting a seed, nut, ice cube, or crusty bread. Others develop a dull ache once the nerve becomes more irritated.

When to Call Promptly

Schedule an evaluation if biting causes repeatable pain, a tooth becomes newly sensitive, or a piece of tooth breaks away. Call urgently for swelling, severe constant pain, fever, drainage, or a bad taste, since these can indicate infection.

Why Teeth Crack

Teeth can crack after biting a hard object, an accident, or years of grinding and clenching. Large fillings may leave less natural tooth structure to absorb chewing forces. Age-related wear, rapid temperature changes, and an uneven bite can also contribute. Sometimes there is no single dramatic event. A small fracture develops gradually under repeated stress.

Not every visible line is dangerous. Tiny enamel lines called craze lines are common and usually cosmetic. A true crack extends deeper into the tooth and can allow bacteria or movement to irritate the pulp. The depth and direction of that crack determine whether the tooth can be restored.

How a Dentist Finds the Crack

Diagnosis starts with listening closely to when and how the pain occurs. We examine the tooth, check the bite, test individual cusps, evaluate temperature response, and inspect the surrounding gums. Magnification, special lighting, staining, and dental X-rays may provide additional clues.

Because a narrow crack may not show on an X-ray, the diagnosis often comes from combining several findings. The goal is to distinguish a cracked tooth from decay, a loose filling, gum problems, sinus pressure, or pain referred from another tooth.

Can a Cracked Tooth Be Saved?

Many cracked teeth can be saved when treated early. A small damaged cusp may be repaired with a filling or onlay. A crown is often used to hold a more extensively cracked tooth together and protect it from flexing during chewing.

If the crack has inflamed or infected the nerve, root canal treatment may be needed before the final crown. Root canal treatment addresses the damaged tissue inside the tooth, while the crown helps protect the tooth structurally. If a crack extends far below the gumline or splits the tooth into separate pieces, extraction may be the only predictable option. An exam is the only way to know which situation applies.

What to Do Until Your Appointment

Avoid chewing hard or crunchy foods on the painful side. Do not test the tooth repeatedly, since extra pressure may worsen the crack. Keep the area clean with gentle brushing and flossing. If a piece breaks off, save it in a clean container and bring it with you.

Do not place aspirin directly on the tooth or gum. If you can safely take over-the-counter pain medicine, follow the package directions. These steps may reduce discomfort, but they cannot seal or stabilize a crack.

Early Evaluation Gives You More Options

A crack does not heal like a broken bone. Once tooth structure separates, the crack can remain stable or continue to spread. Treating it before constant pain or infection develops usually gives us more options and may require less extensive care.

If you have intermittent pain when chewing, do not wait for the tooth to break. A focused exam can identify the cause and help protect your smile, comfort, and ability to eat normally.
