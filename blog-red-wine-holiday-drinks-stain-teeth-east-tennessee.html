Does Red Wine Stain Your Teeth? Holiday Drinks and Your Smile

Holiday parties bring red wine, coffee, cranberry, and dark sodas. Here is how to enjoy them without a dull, stained smile in January.

Published November 24, 2026  |  Elite Dental

From Thanksgiving through New Year's, most of us drink more red wine, coffee, cider, and sweet holiday drinks than any other time of year. Patients at our Dandridge and Jefferson City offices often notice in January that their teeth look a shade or two darker than they did in the fall. That is not your imagination.

The good news is that most drink stains are preventable, and the ones that do build up are usually easy to treat. A few small habits make a big difference.

The Short Version

Sip water between drinks to rinse away color and acid.
Wait 30 to 60 minutes to brush after wine, since acid softens enamel.
Eat while you drink. Food and saliva protect your teeth.
Keep your cleaning on the calendar. Surface stains usually polish right off.

Why Red Wine Stains Teeth So Easily

Red wine stains teeth through a combination of three things working together.

Color. Red wine is full of deeply pigmented compounds that come from grape skins. These pigments are what give the wine its color, and they cling to anything they touch, including your enamel.

Tannins. Tannins are the compounds that make red wine feel dry in your mouth. They help pigment bind to the thin protein film that naturally coats your teeth, so color sticks instead of rinsing away.

Acid. Wine is acidic. Acid temporarily softens and roughens the enamel surface, which gives stains more places to grab on. This is why white wine, which has very little color, can still make teeth stain faster: it etches the surface so the next cup of coffee leaves more behind.

The Other Holiday Drinks That Stain

Red wine gets the blame, but it has plenty of company this time of year:

  • Coffee and tea. Daily coffee is the most common cause of staining we see. Our article on coffee stains on teeth covers it in detail.
  • Cranberry juice and sangria. Dark, acidic, and often sweet. That is a triple hit.
  • Cola and dark sodas. Color plus acid plus sugar.
  • Spiced cider and mulled wine. Usually served warm and sipped slowly, which keeps teeth exposed for longer.
  • Sweet cocktails and eggnog. Less staining, but lots of sugar that feeds cavity-causing bacteria.

How long you sip matters as much as what you sip. A glass of wine with dinner is easier on your teeth than one glass nursed over three hours at a party, because every sip restarts the acid exposure.

Simple Ways to Prevent Stains

Alternate with water. A sip of water between drinks rinses pigment and acid before they settle. It is the easiest habit with the biggest payoff.

Eat with your drinks. Chewing increases saliva, which neutralizes acid and washes your teeth. Cheese is a great pairing for wine because it helps balance acid in the mouth.

Do not brush right away. It sounds backward, but brushing immediately after wine or other acidic drinks can scrub softened enamel. Rinse with water, then wait 30 to 60 minutes before brushing.

Brush before the party. Plaque gives stains something extra to hold onto. Clean teeth stain less, so brushing and flossing before you go out actually helps.

Chew sugar-free gum afterward. It gets saliva flowing and helps clear the mouth when you cannot brush yet.

Watch Out for Dry Mouth

Alcohol dries out your mouth, and saliva is your natural defense against both stains and cavities. If you also take medications that cause dry mouth, holiday drinking can tip the balance. Drink extra water, especially before bed.

When Stains Have Already Set In

If your teeth already look darker than you would like, you have good options.

Professional cleaning. Your hygienist removes plaque, tartar, and surface stain and polishes the enamel smooth. Many patients are surprised how much brighter their teeth look after a routine cleaning.

Professional whitening. For deeper stains, professional teeth whitening lifts discoloration from inside the enamel. It is safer and more predictable than most store-bought kits. If your teeth tend to be sensitive, read our tips on sensitivity after whitening.

Crowns, veneers, and fillings. Whitening does not change the color of dental work. If you have visible crowns or tooth-colored fillings, see our guide on whitening around crowns, fillings, and veneers before you start.

When a Stain Is Not Just a Stain

Most drink stains are even and spread across several teeth. A single tooth that turns gray or dark, a brown spot that feels sticky or rough, or a dark line at the edge of a filling should be checked. Those can be signs of decay, a leaking filling, or a problem inside the tooth, and whitening will not fix them. Frequent acidic drinks can also wear enamel over time. Common Questions

Should I brush my teeth right after drinking red wine?

No. Wine is acidic and softens enamel for a little while. Brushing right away can scrub that softened surface. Rinse with water, then wait about 30 to 60 minutes before you brush.

Does white wine stain teeth?

White wine does not carry much color on its own, but it is just as acidic as red. That acid roughens enamel so other stains, like coffee or red sauce later in the day, stick more easily. It also adds to enamel wear over time.

Can whitening remove red wine stains?

Usually, yes. Surface stains often come off with a professional cleaning, and deeper staining in the enamel responds well to professional whitening. Whitening does not change the color of crowns, veneers, or tooth-colored fillings, so we plan around any existing dental work.
