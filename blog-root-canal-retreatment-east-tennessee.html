Can a Root Canal Fail Years Later?

Most root canal-treated teeth last for many years, but new pain or infection can occasionally develop.

Published September 24, 2026  |  Elite Dental

Root canal treatment removes infected or damaged tissue from inside a tooth, cleans the internal spaces, and seals them. The tooth is then restored, often with a crown, so it can function normally. Treatment is highly successful, but no dental procedure can guarantee that a tooth will remain problem-free forever.

A previously treated tooth may become uncomfortable months or even years later. This does not always mean the original treatment was performed incorrectly. Teeth have complex anatomy, restorations wear over time, and new problems can expose the tooth to bacteria.

Why Can a Root Canal-Treated Tooth Hurt Again?

Some roots contain very narrow, curved, or branching canals that are difficult to detect during the first procedure. If bacteria remain in an untreated space, infection may persist quietly before symptoms appear. In other cases, a delayed crown or a restoration that no longer seals properly allows bacteria to reenter the tooth.

New decay can form around a filling or crown. A tooth can also develop a fracture from heavy biting forces, teeth grinding, or years of use. Occasionally, the discomfort comes from the gum, a neighboring tooth, sinus pressure, or a bite problem rather than the root canal itself. An examination is needed to identify the source.

Signs That Deserve an Evaluation

Contact a dentist if a root canal-treated tooth becomes tender when you bite, develops persistent pressure or throbbing, or causes swelling in the gum or face. A small pimple-like bump on the gum can be a drainage point from an infection, even if it is not painful. A bad taste, loose crown, or visible crack also warrants attention.

A brief, mild sensation does not always signal failure, especially soon after treatment. Pain that increases, repeatedly returns, or lasts more than a few days should not be ignored. Because the nerve inside the tooth was removed, an infection can sometimes progress with surprisingly little discomfort.

When Is It an Emergency?

Call promptly for facial swelling, fever, difficulty swallowing, or rapidly worsening pain. Significant swelling that affects breathing or swallowing requires emergency medical care. Dental antibiotics may calm a spreading infection, but they do not remove its source. Definitive dental treatment is usually still necessary.

How the Tooth Is Checked

Your dentist will review when the root canal was completed, how the tooth was restored, and when the current symptoms began. The examination may include checking the bite, tapping or pressing around the tooth, evaluating the crown, testing nearby teeth, and taking dental X-rays. Three-dimensional imaging may be helpful when ordinary X-rays do not reveal the full root anatomy or the location of a suspected problem.

The goal is to determine whether the tooth can be predictably saved. The amount of healthy tooth structure, condition of the bone and gums, location of any crack, and quality of the existing restoration all affect that decision.

What Is Root Canal Retreatment?

During nonsurgical retreatment, the tooth is reopened and the old root canal filling material is removed. The canals are carefully examined, cleaned, disinfected, and sealed again. A new permanent restoration is then needed to protect the tooth. Complex cases are often treated by an endodontist, a dentist with advanced training in root canal procedures.

If infection remains near the root tip and conventional retreatment is not appropriate, an endodontic surgery called an apicoectomy may be considered. The infected tissue and a small portion of the root tip are removed through the gum, then the end of the root is sealed.

When Extraction May Be the Better Choice

Retreatment cannot save every tooth. A vertical root fracture, extensive decay below the gumline, severe bone loss, or too little remaining tooth structure may make extraction more predictable. The missing tooth can then be replaced with a dental implant, bridge, or removable option. Your dentist should explain the expected lifespan, cost, and tradeoffs of saving versus replacing the tooth.

Protecting a Root Canal-Treated Tooth

Keep the crown and gumline clean with daily brushing and interdental cleaning. Attend regular exams so decay, a loose restoration, or bite wear can be found early. If you grind your teeth, a custom night guard may reduce damaging forces. Avoid chewing ice and using teeth to open packages.

New pain does not automatically mean the tooth must be removed. Early evaluation gives you the best chance of finding a treatable cause and preserving the tooth.
