Cold and Flu Season: Protecting Your Teeth When You Are Sick

When you feel awful, your teeth are the last thing on your mind. A few small habits keep a week on the couch from turning into a cavity.

Published October 7, 2026  |  Elite Dental

Every winter we see the same pattern. A patient comes in for a routine cleaning and has new soft spots on teeth that were healthy six months ago. When we ask what changed, the answer is often a long cold, a round of the flu, or a stubborn sinus infection. The illness itself rarely harms teeth. The things we do to feel better while we are sick often do.

At Elite Dental, we care for families from Dandridge, Jefferson City, White Pine, Morristown, and across East Tennessee. Here is what to watch for this cold and flu season, and the simple habits that protect your teeth until you are back on your feet.

Cough Drops Are Candy With a Job Title

Most regular cough drops and throat lozenges are sweetened with sugar or corn syrup. On their own, one or two would not matter much. The problem is how we use them. When you are sick, it is easy to keep one in your mouth all day, unwrapping a new drop as soon as the last one dissolves.

That is the worst possible pattern for teeth. Each drop feeds the bacteria in plaque, which produce acid that pulls minerals out of enamel. A slow-dissolving drop keeps that acid attack going for a long time, and back-to-back drops never give saliva a chance to repair the damage.

Better choice: look for sugar-free lozenges, ideally ones sweetened with xylitol. They soothe the throat without feeding cavity-causing bacteria.

Cold Medicine and Dry Mouth

Saliva is your mouth's built-in defense system. It rinses away food, neutralizes acid, and delivers minerals that rebuild weak spots in enamel. Many common cold and allergy medicines, especially decongestants and antihistamines, reduce saliva flow. Add a stuffy nose that forces you to breathe through your mouth, especially at night, and your mouth can stay dry for days.

A dry mouth is a cavity-friendly mouth. To counter it:

Sip water throughout the day. Staying hydrated helps your body fight the illness and keeps saliva flowing.

Try a humidifier at night. If congestion has you sleeping with your mouth open, moist air can make a real difference.

Chew sugar-free gum. If you feel up to it, a piece of xylitol gum stimulates saliva.

Watch the Liquid Medicine

Many liquid cold and cough medicines, including most children's versions, are sweetened to make them easier to swallow. That syrup coats the teeth. When possible, give liquid medicine at mealtime, follow it with a drink of water, and avoid giving a sugary dose right after the bedtime brush. If your child needs medicine at night, have them rinse with water afterward. Always follow your doctor or pharmacist on dosing and timing first.

The Sick-Day Short List

Choose sugar-free lozenges. Drink water between everything else. Rinse after syrups. Wait 30 minutes to brush after vomiting. Keep brushing twice a day, even when you feel terrible.

Sports Drinks, Juice, and Tea With Honey

Fluids are important when you are sick, but not all fluids are kind to teeth. Sports drinks, orange juice, and lemon-flavored drinks combine sugar with acid. Hot tea with honey and lemon is soothing, but sipped slowly all day it works much like a cough drop.

Water should be your main drink. If your doctor recommends an electrolyte drink, drink it with a meal or in one sitting rather than sipping for hours, and follow it with water.

If You Are Throwing Up

Stomach acid is strong enough to soften enamel. Your first instinct may be to brush right away, but brushing softened enamel can scrub some of it off. Instead, rinse with water, or with a cup of water mixed with a teaspoon of baking soda to neutralize the acid, then wait about 30 minutes before brushing. We cover this in more detail in our guide on protecting your teeth after vomiting.

Do Not Skip Brushing

When you can barely get off the couch, brushing feels optional. It is not. Plaque builds up just as fast when you are sick, and with dry mouth and extra sugar in the mix, it does more damage. Brush for two minutes with fluoride toothpaste twice a day and clean between your teeth once a day. If even that feels like too much, a fluoride rinse before bed is far better than nothing.

What About Your Toothbrush?

Replacing your toothbrush after an illness is a reasonable precaution, especially if it is close to its three-month replacement date anyway. More important: never share a toothbrush, store family brushes so the heads do not touch, and let them air dry upright instead of sealing them in a closed cover while wet.

When Sinus Pressure Feels Like a Toothache

The roots of the upper back teeth sit right beneath the sinuses. When the sinuses swell, the pressure can feel exactly like a toothache, often in several teeth at once and worse when you bend over. This kind of pain usually fades as the congestion clears. If one tooth keeps hurting after you feel better, or if you notice swelling in your gums or face, call us. Common Questions

Should I replace my toothbrush after being sick?

It is a reasonable precaution once you recover, especially if the bristles are worn, but the most important rule is never to share a toothbrush and to keep family brushes from touching.

Are cough drops bad for your teeth?

Most regular cough drops contain sugar and dissolve slowly, which bathes the teeth in sugar for a long time. Sugar-free lozenges are a better choice.

Can cold medicine cause cavities?

Decongestants and antihistamines often cause dry mouth, and less saliva means less protection against cavities. Sugary syrups add to the risk.

Should I brush right after throwing up?

No. Rinse with water or a mix of water and baking soda, then wait about 30 minutes before brushing so softened enamel is not scrubbed away.

Why do my upper teeth hurt when I have a sinus infection?

The roots of the upper back teeth sit close to the sinuses, so sinus pressure can feel like a toothache. If the pain stays after the congestion clears, have the tooth checked.
