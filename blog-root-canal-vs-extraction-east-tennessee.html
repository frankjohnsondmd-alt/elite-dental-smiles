Root Canal or Extraction? How to Decide

When a tooth is badly infected, you usually have two choices: save it or remove it. Here is how to weigh cost, comfort, and what happens years down the road.

Published October 13, 2026  |  Elite Dental

“Can we just pull it?” We hear that question almost every week, usually from someone who is in pain, worried about cost, and wants the problem gone. It is a fair question. Sometimes extraction really is the right call. But often it is the choice that costs more and causes more trouble later.

When the nerve inside a tooth is infected or dying, the tooth will not heal on its own. The two main options are a root canal, which saves the tooth, or an extraction, which removes it. Here is how we help patients in Dandridge, Jefferson City, and across East Tennessee make that decision.

What Each Option Actually Involves

A root canal removes the infected or inflamed pulp from inside the tooth, cleans and disinfects the canals, and seals them. Most back teeth then need a crown to protect them, because a tooth that has had a root canal is more likely to crack. With modern numbing, the procedure usually feels about like getting a filling. The pain people associate with root canals is almost always the infection beforehand, not the treatment.

An extraction removes the whole tooth. It is often faster on the day of treatment and usually costs less up front. The infection is gone once the tooth is out and the area heals.

The catch is what comes after. An extraction ends one problem, but it starts a new decision: what to do about the gap.

What Happens After a Tooth Is Removed

Teeth work as a team. When one is lost, the rest of the mouth slowly adjusts, and not in a good way:

Neighboring teeth drift and tip into the open space, which can create food traps, bite problems, and gum pockets that are hard to clean.

The opposing tooth can over-erupt, slowly growing down or up into the gap because nothing is stopping it.

The jawbone shrinks. Bone that no longer supports a tooth gradually resorbs. That can make a future implant harder and may mean a bone graft is needed first.

Chewing shifts to the other side, which puts extra wear on the remaining teeth.

A single missing tooth in the very back of the mouth is sometimes fine to leave alone, depending on your bite. But in most cases, a missing tooth should be replaced with an implant, a bridge, or a partial denture to keep the rest of the mouth stable.

Compare the Full Cost, Not Just Today's Cost

An extraction is usually cheaper on day one. Once you add an implant or bridge to replace the tooth, the total is often higher than a root canal and crown. If you are comparing prices, compare the whole path, not just the first appointment.

When Saving the Tooth Makes Sense

A root canal is usually the better choice when:

The tooth has enough healthy structure left to support a crown.

The gums and bone around the tooth are healthy, so the tooth is stable.

The root is not cracked. A crack that runs down the root usually cannot be fixed.

The tooth is important for chewing or your smile, such as a front tooth or a first molar.

With a good seal and a well-fitting crown, a tooth that has had a root canal can last for many years, often for life. Nothing we place in the mouth works quite as well as your own natural tooth.

When Extraction Is the Better Choice

Saving a tooth is not always realistic or wise. Extraction is often recommended when:

The tooth is broken below the gumline or so badly decayed that there is not enough left to restore.

The root is fractured. A vertical root fracture is one of the most common reasons a tooth cannot be saved.

Advanced gum disease has destroyed the supporting bone and the tooth is very loose.

It is a wisdom tooth or another tooth that does not play a real role in chewing.

A previous root canal has failed and retreatment is unlikely to succeed.

In these cases, removing the tooth and planning a replacement is often the more predictable path. If an implant is the goal, we can sometimes place a bone graft at the time of extraction to preserve the site.

What to Avoid: Waiting It Out

The one choice that never works well is doing nothing. Sometimes an infected tooth stops hurting, and it is tempting to think the problem went away. Usually it means the nerve has died and the infection is quietly spreading into the bone. A dental abscess can cause facial swelling and, in rare cases, serious illness. Antibiotics may calm a flare-up, but they do not fix the tooth. The infection comes back until the source is treated.

If you have swelling in your face or jaw, fever, or trouble swallowing or breathing, seek emergency care right away.

How We Help You Decide

At Elite Dental, we start with an exam and X-rays to see how much healthy tooth remains, what the bone looks like, and whether there are any cracks. When the picture is not clear, 3D imaging can show details a regular X-ray misses. Then we walk you through both options with honest pros, cons, and costs, including what replacing the tooth would involve if you choose extraction.

Our doctors perform root canals, extractions, and dental implants, so you get a recommendation based on what is best for the tooth, not on which procedure we happen to offer. For anxious patients, sedation options are available for either treatment.

Common Questions

Is a root canal more painful than an extraction?

No. With modern anesthesia, most patients say a root canal feels similar to getting a filling. Both procedures are done with the area fully numb, and soreness afterward is usually mild and short-lived.

Is it cheaper to pull a tooth than get a root canal?

On the day of treatment, yes. But if the missing tooth needs to be replaced with an implant or bridge, the total cost is often higher than a root canal and crown.

How long does a tooth last after a root canal?

With a proper crown and good home care, a tooth that has had a root canal can last many years and often a lifetime.
