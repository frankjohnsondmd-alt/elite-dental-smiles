Brushing and Flossing With Arthritis: Easier Ways to Keep Your Teeth Healthy

When your hands hurt, a toothbrush can feel like a chore. A few simple changes make daily care much easier, and your teeth will thank you for it.

Published November 8, 2026  |  Elite Dental

Many of our patients in Dandridge, Jefferson City, and across East Tennessee live with arthritis. Whether it is osteoarthritis in the fingers, rheumatoid arthritis, or stiffness in the shoulders and wrists, the effect on oral care is the same: gripping a thin toothbrush handle and winding floss around your fingers can be painful or nearly impossible. Over time, missed spots turn into plaque buildup, gum inflammation, and cavities along the gumline.

The good news is that you do not need perfect hand strength to keep a healthy mouth. You need the right tools and a routine that works with your body instead of against it.

The Short Version

Easiest toothbrush: an electric brush with a wide handle and a two-minute timer.
Easiest flossing: long-handled floss holders, floss picks, interdental brushes, or a water flosser.
Extra protection: prescription-strength fluoride toothpaste and more frequent cleanings.
Tell us about your medications: some arthritis drugs cause dry mouth, which raises cavity risk.

Why Arthritis Makes Oral Care Harder

Brushing well takes more fine motor control than most people realize. You have to hold the handle at an angle, make small circular motions, and reach the back of your mouth, all while keeping steady pressure. Arthritis can limit each of those steps. Pain in the fingers makes a tight grip hard. Stiff wrists make it difficult to angle the brush toward the gumline. Shoulder pain can make it hard to keep your arm raised for two full minutes.

Flossing is often the first thing to go, because wrapping string around two fingers and guiding it between back teeth demands the most dexterity of all. Unfortunately, the areas between teeth are exactly where gum disease and cavities tend to start.

Make Brushing Easier

Switch to an electric toothbrush

An electric toothbrush does the scrubbing for you. Your job is simply to hold it against each tooth for a couple of seconds and move on. Most models also have thicker handles, which are easier to grip, and a built-in timer so you do not have to count. We compare options in our article on electric versus manual toothbrushes.

Build up the handle

If you prefer a manual brush, make the handle wider. You can slide it into a foam hair curler, wrap it with a bicycle grip or a tennis ball cut with a slit, or buy a toothbrush designed with a large, rubberized handle. A wider handle spreads the pressure across your whole hand instead of your fingertips.

Change your position

Sit down while you brush and rest your elbow on the counter. This takes strain off your shoulder and gives you a steadier hand. If your arm tires, brush one half of your mouth, take a short break, and then finish the other half.

Choose easier packaging

Small toothpaste caps can be surprisingly hard to open. Flip-top caps or pump dispensers solve this problem, and they are inexpensive.

Make Flossing Easier

If traditional floss is not working for you, that is fine. What matters is that the spaces between your teeth get cleaned, not which tool does it.

  • Long-handled floss holders hold the floss tight for you, so you only need to guide it between teeth.
  • Disposable floss picks are small and simple. Look for ones with a longer handle if reaching your back teeth is difficult.
  • Interdental brushes are tiny bristle brushes that slide between teeth. Many people with arthritis find them easier to control than floss.
  • Water flossers use a stream of water to rinse between teeth and along the gumline. You just point and move slowly. See our article on water flossers versus floss.

Watch for Medication Side Effects

Arthritis treatment often involves daily medications, and some of them affect the mouth. Certain drugs can cause dry mouth, mouth sores, or changes in the gums. Dry mouth is the biggest concern, because saliva is your natural defense against cavities. With less of it, decay can develop quickly, especially around older fillings and crowns. Learn more in our article on medications that cause dry mouth.

Some people with rheumatoid arthritis also have Sjögren's syndrome, a related condition that significantly reduces saliva. Research has also found a connection between rheumatoid arthritis and gum disease, since both involve chronic inflammation. Keeping your gums healthy is one more way to support your overall health.

If you are planning a joint replacement, talk with your surgeon and with us ahead of time. Many surgeons want dental problems addressed before surgery. Our guide to dental clearance before joint replacement explains what to expect.

How Your Dental Team Can Help

We adjust care for patients who have trouble brushing and flossing at home. Depending on your situation, we may recommend:

  • Prescription-strength fluoride toothpaste to strengthen enamel and reduce cavity risk.
  • Fluoride varnish applied at your cleaning visits.
  • More frequent cleanings, such as every three or four months instead of every six, to remove buildup before it causes problems.
  • Product suggestions tailored to your hands, mouth, and any dental work you have.

If arthritis affects your jaw, which can happen with both osteoarthritis and rheumatoid arthritis, let us know. Jaw stiffness can make long appointments uncomfortable, and we can plan shorter visits or more breaks. Our article on jaw popping and TMJ covers jaw joint symptoms in more detail. For more on staying healthy as you age, read our guide to dental care for seniors.

Common Questions

What is the best toothbrush for someone with arthritis?

For most people with arthritis, an electric toothbrush with a wide, cushioned handle and a built-in two-minute timer is the easiest option. It does the scrubbing motion for you, so you only need to guide it along your teeth.

How can I floss if I cannot wrap floss around my fingers?

Try a floss holder with a long handle, disposable floss picks, a water flosser, or small interdental brushes. All of them clean between teeth without needing fine finger control.

Can arthritis medications affect my teeth?

Yes. Some medications used for arthritis and related conditions can cause dry mouth, mouth sores, or gum changes, and dry mouth raises cavity risk. Bring an updated medication list to every dental visit so we can adjust your care.
