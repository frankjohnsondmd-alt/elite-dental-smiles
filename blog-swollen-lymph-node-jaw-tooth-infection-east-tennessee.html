Swollen Lymph Node Under Your Jaw? When a Tooth Is the Cause

A tender lump under the jaw is often your body fighting an infection nearby. Sometimes that infection is in a tooth or the gums.

Published November 25, 2026  |  Elite Dental

You run your fingers along your jaw and find a sore, pea-sized lump just under the jawbone. Most people assume it is a cold or a sore throat, and often it is. But at our Dandridge and Jefferson City offices, we regularly see patients whose swollen lymph node turned out to be a sign of a tooth or gum infection they did not know they had.

Here is how to tell the difference, what to watch for, and when to call.

The Short Version

Lymph nodes swell when they are fighting infection in the area they drain, and the nodes under your jaw drain your teeth and gums.
A node on one side plus a sore tooth, swollen gum, or bad taste points toward a dental cause.
A node that lasts more than two weeks with no clear reason should be checked.
Facial swelling, fever, or trouble swallowing or breathing needs care the same day.

What Lymph Nodes Do

Lymph nodes are small filters spread throughout the body. When germs show up in the area a node drains, immune cells gather there and the node swells. That swelling is a sign your body is doing its job.

The nodes tucked under your lower jaw, called the submandibular nodes, drain the teeth, gums, tongue, and cheeks. The nodes just under the chin drain the lower front teeth and lip. So when something is brewing in your mouth, these are often the first nodes to react.

Dental Problems That Can Swell a Lymph Node

A tooth abscess. When decay or a crack lets bacteria reach the nerve inside a tooth, infection can form at the tip of the root. Nearby nodes often swell as the body tries to contain it. Learn more about tooth abscess signs.

Gum infections. A gum abscess or a pimple-like bump on the gums, sometimes called a gum boil, can drain into the lymph nodes. Advanced gum disease can also keep nodes mildly swollen.

Wisdom teeth. A partly erupted wisdom tooth can trap food and bacteria under a flap of gum. The area gets red and sore, it may hurt to open wide, and the node under that side of the jaw often swells. See our guide to wisdom teeth.

Signs It Is Probably Your Tooth

A dental cause is more likely when the swollen node is on one side and you also notice any of these:

  • A tooth that aches, throbs, or hurts when you bite
  • Lingering pain with hot or cold
  • A swollen gum around one tooth
  • A bad taste or bad breath that does not go away
  • A small bump on the gum that drains
  • Pain or swelling near a back molar or wisdom tooth

Keep in mind that some tooth infections do not hurt much, especially if the nerve has already died. A swollen node can be the only obvious clue.

Signs It Is Probably Something Else

Colds, strep throat, ear infections, and other viruses are very common causes of swollen nodes, especially in children. With those, you usually have a sore throat, congestion, or fever, and the nodes on both sides of the neck may be tender. They typically shrink within a week or two as you recover.

A swelling right under the jaw that gets bigger or more painful when you eat may be a salivary gland problem, such as a small stone blocking the gland. That is different from a lymph node, and it is worth having examined.

When to Call Us

Call your dentist if a swollen node comes with any tooth or gum symptoms, or if it does not go away within about two weeks. Dental infections do not heal on their own. Antibiotics may calm things down for a while, but the source has to be treated, usually with a root canal, a deep cleaning, or removing the tooth.

Also have a node checked if it is hard, does not move when you press on it, is not tender, or keeps growing. Most of the time these turn out to be harmless, but they are worth ruling out. Your routine visits include an oral cancer screening where we check the head and neck, including these nodes.

When It Is an Emergency

Dental infections can spread into the face and neck. Get care right away if you have:

  • Swelling in your face, cheek, or under your jaw that is spreading
  • Fever with a toothache
  • Trouble opening your mouth or swallowing
  • Swelling under the tongue or in the floor of the mouth

Call us the same day for emergency dental care. If you have any trouble breathing, go to the nearest emergency room or call 911.

What to Do in the Meantime

Rinse with warm salt water a few times a day, keep brushing and flossing gently, and use an over-the-counter pain reliever as directed if you need one. A cold compress on the outside of the jaw can ease soreness. Do not put heat on facial swelling, and do not try to pop or drain anything yourself. These steps can make you more comfortable, but they do not replace an exam.

Common Questions

Can a tooth infection cause a swollen lymph node in the neck?

Yes. The nodes under the jaw and in the upper neck drain the teeth and gums. An abscessed tooth, gum infection, or inflamed wisdom tooth commonly makes those nodes swollen and tender, usually on the same side as the problem.

How long does a lymph node stay swollen after a tooth infection is treated?

Once the infection is treated, a swollen node usually starts to shrink within a few days and is often back to normal in one to two weeks. If it stays enlarged longer than that, let us know so we can take another look.

Should I see a dentist or a doctor for a swollen lymph node?

If you also have tooth pain, gum swelling, a bad taste, or a sore wisdom tooth, start with your dentist. If you have a sore throat, congestion, or other cold symptoms and your teeth feel fine, your doctor is a good first stop. Either way, a node that lasts more than two weeks should be checked.
