Why Does My Tooth Hurt When I Fly?

Cabin pressure changes can expose a problem that was already developing inside a tooth or sinus.

Published September 11, 2026  |  Elite Dental

A toothache that appears during takeoff or landing can be confusing, especially when the tooth felt normal on the ground. The pressure change did not usually create a new dental problem in a few minutes. More often, it made an existing problem noticeable.

Dentists sometimes call pressure-related tooth pain barodontalgia. It can affect airline passengers, pilots, divers, and anyone exposed to rapid pressure changes. At Elite Dental, we help patients from Dandridge, Jefferson City, and surrounding East Tennessee communities identify the actual source before the next trip.

How Flying Can Trigger Tooth Pain

Commercial airplane cabins are pressurized, but cabin pressure still changes as the aircraft climbs and descends. Healthy teeth do not contain open pockets of air that should expand painfully. A tooth with decay, a crack, a loose filling, an inflamed nerve, or infection around its root may respond differently to those changes.

Pressure can also affect the sinuses. The roots of upper back teeth sit close to the maxillary sinuses, so sinus pressure may feel exactly like a toothache. Congestion from a cold, allergies, or a sinus infection makes this more likely.

Clues About the Possible Cause

Sharp pain in one specific tooth may point toward decay, a leaking restoration, or a crack. A lingering ache after cold or heat can suggest inflammation inside the tooth. Pain when biting may occur with a crack or infection near the root. A dull ache across several upper teeth, especially with congestion or facial pressure, may be related to the sinus.

These clues are useful, but they cannot confirm a diagnosis. Dental and sinus symptoms overlap, and a tooth can have significant damage without hurting between flights.

Flying Usually Reveals Rather Than Causes the Problem

If a tooth hurts at altitude, schedule an exam even if the pain disappears after landing. Temporary relief does not prove the tooth is healthy.

What to Do During the Flight

Note whether the pain begins during ascent, cruising, or descent and whether it affects one tooth or a broader area. Avoid very hot, cold, or sugary foods if they trigger the tooth. Over-the-counter pain medicine may help if it is normally safe for you and taken according to its label.

Do not place aspirin directly on the gum because it can injure the tissue. If you are congested, discuss safe options with your physician or pharmacist, particularly if you have high blood pressure, take other medicines, or have health conditions that limit decongestant use.

How We Find the Source

A dental evaluation may include checking existing fillings and crowns, testing the tooth's response to temperature, examining the bite, and looking for cracks or gum swelling. Dental X-rays can reveal decay, changes near a root, or other problems that are not visible during a basic inspection.

If the teeth appear healthy and symptoms fit a sinus pattern, medical evaluation may be appropriate. The goal is to avoid treating the wrong source while making sure a quiet dental infection is not missed.

Treatment Depends on the Diagnosis

A small cavity may need a filling. A damaged or loose restoration may need repair or replacement. A deeply inflamed or infected pulp may require root canal treatment, followed by the appropriate restoration. A cracked tooth may need a crown, root canal treatment, or extraction depending on the depth and location of the fracture.

If sinus pressure is responsible, dental treatment will not solve it. That distinction is one reason an examination matters more than trying to judge the cause from altitude alone.

Should You Fly With a Recent Dental Procedure?

Routine fillings and uncomplicated dental work do not automatically prevent travel. However, symptoms, surgical recovery, active infection, and the complexity of treatment vary. Ask your dentist before flying after an extraction, implant surgery, sinus-related procedure, or urgent treatment. Follow the specific recovery instructions you receive.

If travel is approaching and you have unexplained sensitivity, a broken tooth, swelling, or intermittent pain, schedule a visit before departure. Addressing a manageable problem at home is far easier than finding urgent dental care during a trip.

When to Seek Urgent Care

Contact a dentist promptly for facial swelling, fever, drainage, severe pain, or difficulty biting. Seek emergency medical care if swelling affects breathing or swallowing or spreads toward the eye or neck. These signs can indicate infection beyond the tooth and should not wait for a convenient travel schedule.

A tooth that hurts only in the air is still giving useful information. An exam can determine whether the source is decay, a restoration, the tooth's nerve, a crack, or nearby sinus pressure, helping you travel with fewer surprises.
