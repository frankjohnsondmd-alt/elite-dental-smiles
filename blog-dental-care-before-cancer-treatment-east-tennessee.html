Dental Care Before Cancer Treatment

A dental visit before chemotherapy or radiation is one of the easiest ways to avoid an interruption later.

Published October 5, 2026  |  Elite Dental

A cancer diagnosis fills a calendar fast. Scans, labs, port placement, treatment planning. Then the oncology office adds one more item that can feel out of place: see your dentist before treatment starts. It is not busywork. A dental problem that is quiet today can become a serious complication once chemotherapy or radiation is underway.

At Elite Dental, we see patients from Dandridge, Jefferson City, White Pine, Morristown, and the surrounding East Tennessee communities for pretreatment dental evaluations. Here is why the visit matters, what we look at, and how to keep your mouth comfortable through treatment.

Why Cancer Treatment Changes the Stakes

Chemotherapy works by targeting rapidly dividing cells, and the lining of your mouth is made of rapidly dividing cells. That is why mouth sores, sensitivity, and taste changes are common side effects. Many chemotherapy regimens also lower white blood cell counts for stretches of time, which reduces your ability to contain an infection that your body would normally handle without much trouble.

Radiation to the head or neck adds a different set of concerns. It can reduce saliva production, sometimes permanently, and saliva is a major part of how teeth resist decay. It can also affect the blood supply to the jawbone, which makes healing after an extraction considerably harder in treated areas. For that reason, teeth that need to come out are far better removed before radiation than after.

None of this means dental problems are guaranteed. It means a small issue carries a bigger penalty during treatment than it would otherwise, and the window to address it comfortably is before therapy begins.

What the Pretreatment Visit Covers

Bring your treatment plan or any paperwork from the oncology office, your start date if you have one, and a current medication list. Tell us what kind of cancer is being treated, whether radiation will involve the head or neck, and whether bone-modifying drugs such as bisphosphonates or denosumab are part of the plan. That last detail matters, because those medications carry their own jaw healing considerations.

We then examine your teeth, gums, tongue, and existing dental work, and take X-rays when they are needed to see what an exam cannot. We are looking for active infection, deep decay, failing fillings or crowns, cracked teeth, gum disease, loose teeth, partially erupted wisdom teeth, and sore spots from dentures or partials. A cleaning is often part of the visit, since reducing bacteria and tartar before treatment gives your mouth a better starting point.

Call Before Your Start Date, Not After

Many oncology teams want extraction sites to have at least two weeks of healing before treatment begins, and sometimes longer before head and neck radiation. If a tooth needs to come out, that timeline is the reason to call the day the request is made rather than the week treatment starts.

What Happens If We Find Something

The plan depends on what we find and on how much time there is. Teeth that are infected, unrestorable, or likely to cause trouble during treatment are usually addressed first. Problems that are stable and not at risk of flaring can often wait until after treatment. Elective cosmetic work waits.

We document findings for your oncology team and communicate with them about timing, blood counts, and any precautions they want in place. If your platelet or white cell counts are a factor, your oncologist guides when a procedure is safe. Never stop or restart a prescription on your own, and never delay telling the oncology office about new mouth pain or swelling.

Caring for Your Mouth During Treatment

Keep brushing twice a day with a soft brush and fluoride toothpaste, even if your mouth is tender. Clean between your teeth daily unless your care team has told you otherwise. If brushing becomes painful, switch to an extra soft brush and warm water rather than stopping.

Dry mouth deserves real attention. Sip water through the day, use sugar-free lozenges or a saliva substitute, and run a humidifier at night if your mouth wakes you up. Avoid alcohol-based mouthwashes, which sting and dry tissue further. Many patients benefit from a prescription-strength fluoride, and patients who have had head and neck radiation sometimes use custom fluoride trays long term.

Watch what you reach for when eating is hard. Constant sipping of sweetened drinks or sucking on hard candy for dry mouth relief is a fast route to new cavities in a mouth that has lost saliva protection. Choose sugar-free options.

Call us for mouth sores that keep you from eating or drinking, swelling, a bad taste or drainage, a loose tooth, or pain that is getting worse. Let the oncology office know as well.

Common Questions

Why does my oncologist want a dental exam first?

Cancer treatment can reduce your ability to fight infection and slow healing. The care team wants existing dental problems identified and treated before that window opens.

How early should I schedule?

As soon as the request is made. Two weeks of healing before treatment is a common target, and head and neck radiation may call for more.

Can I have dental work during chemotherapy?

Sometimes, with coordination. Timing around your cycle and blood counts matters, so your dentist and oncologist should agree before any non-urgent procedure.

Will my teeth be affected long term?

Chemotherapy side effects in the mouth usually improve after treatment ends. Head and neck radiation can cause lasting dry mouth, which raises decay risk and makes regular visits and fluoride more important.

What if I am already in treatment and something hurts?

Call us and tell us you are in active cancer treatment. Do not wait it out. We will contact your oncology team about safe timing.

Starting Cancer Treatment Soon?

Call as early as you can so we have time to examine your mouth, handle anything urgent, and coordinate with your oncology team.
