Why Does My Tooth Hurt After Flying?

A flight can expose a dental problem that was already brewing quietly.

Published August 6, 2026  |  Elite Dental

You land from a trip, start driving home through East Tennessee, and suddenly one tooth is aching. Or maybe it started during takeoff or descent, then faded after you got off the plane. Tooth pain after flying can feel strange because nothing obvious happened to your mouth. The flight itself did not damage the tooth, but the pressure change may have revealed a problem that was already there.

Dentists sometimes refer to this as barodontalgia, which simply means tooth pain related to pressure changes. It can happen while flying, scuba diving, or traveling through big elevation changes. Most people do not need to know the technical name. What matters is this: a tooth that hurts during or after a flight deserves attention if the pain lingers, returns, or comes with swelling or sensitivity.

Why Flying Can Make a Tooth Hurt

Air pressure changes inside an airplane cabin as the plane climbs and descends. Healthy teeth usually handle that without any symptoms. If there is inflammation, decay, a crack, or a space under an old filling or crown, pressure changes can irritate the area and make pain show up quickly.

Think of it less as the flight causing the problem and more as the flight stress testing the tooth. A small cavity, irritated nerve, cracked cusp, or leaking restoration may feel quiet on the ground but become noticeable when pressure shifts.

Common Dental Causes

Several dental problems can create tooth pain during or after a flight. The pattern of pain gives useful clues, but an exam is the only way to know for sure.

Deep CavityDecay close to the nerve can flare with pressure changes.
Cracked ToothA crack can trap fluid or air and irritate the nerve.
Old Filling or CrownA gap under dental work may become painful in flight.
Tooth InfectionAn abscess can throb more when pressure shifts.

If the pain is sharp, localized to one tooth, and triggered by biting or cold drinks, the cause is more likely dental than sinus related. If it throbs, wakes you up, or continues after landing, the nerve inside the tooth may be inflamed or infected.

Could It Be Sinus Pressure Instead?

Yes. Sinus pressure is one of the most common non-dental reasons for upper tooth pain after flying. The roots of the upper back teeth sit close to the maxillary sinuses. When those sinuses are congested from allergies, a cold, or a sinus infection, the pressure can feel exactly like a toothache.

Sinus related tooth pain often affects several upper teeth on one side or both sides instead of one specific tooth. It may feel worse when you bend forward, and it often comes with nasal congestion, facial pressure, or recent illness. Still, sinus pressure and dental problems can overlap, so persistent pain should not be guessed at for long.

Pain That Lasts After Landing Is the Key

A quick ache that disappears after the flight may be temporary sinus pressure. Pain that stays, returns when you chew, reacts strongly to cold, or causes swelling is a reason to call a dentist.

When to Call Elite Dental

Call us if your tooth pain lasts more than a day after flying, keeps returning on trips, or feels severe. You should also call promptly if you notice swelling, a bad taste, fever, pain when biting, or sensitivity that lingers after cold or heat. These can point to infection or nerve inflammation.

At Elite Dental, we check the tooth, gums, bite, existing fillings or crowns, and nearby sinus symptoms. Dental X-rays can show decay, infection, bone changes, or problems under old dental work that are not visible from the outside. Once we know the cause, treatment may be as simple as replacing a filling, adjusting a bite, treating gum inflammation, or restoring a cracked tooth. If the nerve is infected, a root canal or extraction may be needed to stop the pain and prevent the infection from spreading.

What to Do Before Your Next Trip

If you have a tooth that has already hurt on a flight, do not wait until the next vacation or business trip to deal with it. Schedule an exam before you travel again. Dental problems are easier to manage at home than from an airport, hotel, or out of state emergency clinic.

If you are flying soon and already have mild dental symptoms, avoid chewing hard foods on that side, keep the area clean, and call for guidance. Over the counter pain relievers may help temporarily if you can take them safely, but they do not fix decay, cracks, or infection.

Common Questions About Tooth Pain After Flying

Why does my tooth hurt after flying?

Cabin pressure changes can aggravate trapped air or inflammation inside a tooth, under a filling, around a crack, or near the sinuses.

Can flying make a cavity hurt?

Yes. A cavity close to the nerve can become more painful during pressure changes, especially during takeoff or landing.

Is tooth pain after flying an emergency?

It can be urgent if pain is severe, lasts after landing, causes swelling, or makes it hard to chew. Those symptoms should be checked promptly.

Can sinus pressure feel like tooth pain?

Yes. Sinus pressure often feels like aching in the upper back teeth, especially after flying with allergies, congestion, or a cold.
