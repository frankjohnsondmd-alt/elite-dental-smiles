Well Water, Fluoride, and Your Family's Teeth

If your house runs on a private well, nobody is adjusting the fluoride in your water. Here is how to find out what you are drinking.

Published October 10, 2026  |  Elite Dental

Drive a few miles outside Dandridge, Jefferson City, or White Pine and a lot of homes stop getting city water. They run on private wells. Well water can be clean, cold, and great tasting, but it comes with one detail most families never think about: nobody is managing the fluoride in it.

City and utility district water is tested and adjusted. A private well is whatever the ground under your house provides. That can mean almost no fluoride, a healthy amount, or more than you want. At Elite Dental, we see patients from across Jefferson, Hamblen, Cocke, and Sevier counties, and the question of what is coming out of the kitchen tap matters more than most people realize, especially for kids.

Why Fluoride in Water Matters

Fluoride strengthens enamel and helps teeth repair the early, microscopic damage that acid causes every time you eat. Small, steady exposure throughout the day is what makes it effective. Drinking water is one of the easiest ways to get that exposure, because you sip it constantly without thinking about it.

The U.S. Public Health Service recommends 0.7 milligrams per liter (mg/L) for community water systems. That number is a good benchmark when you read a well test. It is enough to help prevent cavities while keeping the risk of cosmetic spotting on developing teeth low.

The Problem With Not Knowing

Private wells are not regulated the way public water systems are. The homeowner is responsible for testing, and many families have never tested at all, or tested once when they bought the house and only checked for bacteria.

Fluoride occurs naturally in groundwater, and the level depends on the rock and soil the water passes through. Two wells on neighboring properties can test very differently. So the answer to "does my well have fluoride?" is almost always the same: it depends, and you need a test to know.

When fluoride is too low

Low fluoride is the more common concern. A child growing up on low-fluoride water misses that steady daily protection while their teeth are forming and while new permanent teeth come in. Adults are affected too, particularly anyone with dry mouth, gum recession, or a history of frequent fillings.

When fluoride is too high

Some wells run high. Too much fluoride while permanent teeth are developing, roughly from infancy through age eight, can cause dental fluorosis. Mild fluorosis looks like faint white streaks or flecks and is usually only cosmetic. More severe cases can cause brown staining and pitting. The EPA sets a secondary guideline of 2.0 mg/L specifically because of this cosmetic risk, and an enforceable limit of 4.0 mg/L for public systems. If your well tests above 2.0 mg/L, young children should drink and have their formula mixed with a different water source.

Test the Well Before You Decide Anything

A fluoride test is inexpensive and takes the guesswork out. Contact your county health department or a state-certified water lab, and ask that fluoride be included alongside the usual bacteria and nitrate panel. Bring the results to your next visit and we will walk you through what they mean for each person in your home.

What Your Results Mean

Once you have a number, here is a simple way to think about it:

Below about 0.3 mg/L: Your water is giving your family very little fluoride protection. This is where a conversation about other sources makes sense, especially for children.

Roughly 0.3 to 0.6 mg/L: Some protection, but below the recommended level. Cavity risk and age decide whether anything extra is needed.

Around 0.7 mg/L: Right where you want it. Keep up good brushing habits and regular visits.

Above 2.0 mg/L: Use another water source for young children and for mixing infant formula, and talk with us about what we see on your kids' teeth.

Filters and Bottled Water Change the Picture

Even if your well is in a good range, what you actually drink may not be. Reverse osmosis systems and distillers remove most fluoride. Many households with hard water or iron issues install reverse osmosis at the kitchen sink and never realize it changed their fluoride level. Common pitcher and refrigerator carbon filters generally remove much less.

Bottled water is usually low in fluoride unless the label says it has been added. If your family drinks mostly bottled water, the fluoride in your well may not be reaching anyone's teeth.

How We Fill the Gap When Water Falls Short

Low-fluoride water is easy to make up for. For most families, it starts with the basics: brushing twice a day with fluoride toothpaste, using a rice-grain-sized smear for children under three and a pea-sized amount after that. From there, depending on age and cavity risk, we may recommend:

Fluoride varnish at checkups. A quick, painted-on treatment that takes about a minute and works well for kids and for adults at higher risk.

Dental sealants. Thin protective coatings on the chewing surfaces of back teeth, where most childhood cavities start.

Prescription fluoride. For children whose water is confirmed low and whose risk justifies it, a dietary fluoride supplement may be appropriate. We only recommend this after looking at your water test and every other fluoride source, because adding fluoride on top of a well that is already adequate is how fluorosis happens. Adults with high cavity risk may benefit from a prescription-strength toothpaste instead.

Common Questions

Does well water have fluoride in it?

Most well water contains some natural fluoride, but the amount varies widely. Testing is the only way to know your level.

Can I just give my kids fluoride supplements to be safe?

No. Supplements should only be used when testing confirms low fluoride. Giving them on top of a well that already has enough fluoride raises the risk of fluorosis on permanent teeth.

We moved from city water to a well. Should we change anything?

Test the new well, then tell us at your next visit. Your old water was likely near 0.7 mg/L, and your new water could be quite different.
