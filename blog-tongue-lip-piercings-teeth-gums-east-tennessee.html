Tongue and Lip Piercings: What They Do to Your Teeth and Gums

Oral piercings are common, especially among teens and young adults. Most people never hear about the damage they can quietly cause until a tooth chips or the gums start to pull away.

Published November 15, 2026  |  Elite Dental

We are not here to tell anyone what to do with their body. Plenty of our patients in Dandridge and Jefferson City have tongue, lip, or cheek piercings, and many of them keep healthy smiles for years. But we also see the problems piercings cause often enough that every person who has one, or is thinking about getting one, deserves to know what to watch for.

The short story: the piercing itself is rarely the issue. It is the jewelry rubbing, tapping, and clicking against teeth and gums thousands of times a day that does the damage.

The Short Version

Tongue barbells chip and crack teeth, especially the back of the front teeth and the molars.
Lip rings and studs wear away gum tissue, which can lead to permanent recession.
Shorter, softer jewelry and no playing with it cut the risk a lot.
Regular checkups catch damage while it is still small and cheap to fix.

Chipped and Cracked Teeth

A tongue barbell sits right where your teeth meet. When you talk, eat, or click the ball against your teeth out of habit, the metal strikes enamel with real force. Over months, that adds up to tiny fractures. Sometimes a corner chips off. Other times a crack runs deeper and the tooth starts to hurt when you bite.

The molars take a beating too, because people often bite down on the ball without realizing it, especially while chewing. If you have a tooth that is suddenly sensitive or sharp at the edge, read our guide to cracked tooth symptoms. Small chips can often be repaired with bonding, but a crack that reaches the nerve may need a crown or root canal.

Gum Recession You Cannot Grow Back

Lip piercings, especially on the lower lip, rest against the gums just below the front teeth. The disc on the back of a lip stud or the inside of a ring rubs that thin tissue every time your lip moves. Tongue barbells do the same thing on the inside of the lower front teeth.

Over time, the gum tissue wears down and pulls back, exposing the root. That root surface is softer than enamel, more sensitive to cold, and more prone to decay. Unlike a chipped tooth, gum recession does not heal on its own. Mild cases can be monitored, but advanced recession sometimes needs a gum graft, and in rare cases the front teeth can loosen.

Infection and Swelling

The mouth is full of bacteria, so a new piercing is an open wound in a busy neighborhood. Some swelling for the first week or two is normal, especially with tongue piercings. Call a doctor or dentist right away if you notice:

  • Swelling that keeps getting worse after the first few days
  • Pus, a bad taste, or a foul smell from the site
  • Fever or red streaks spreading from the area
  • Trouble breathing or swallowing, which needs emergency care

Long after healing, jewelry can still collect plaque and tartar just like teeth do, which feeds bad breath and gum irritation if it is not cleaned.

How to Protect Your Teeth if You Keep It

If you love your piercing and plan to keep it, these habits make a real difference:

  • Downsize after healing. Tongue piercings start with a long barbell to allow for swelling. Once healed, a shorter bar sits away from the teeth and is much less likely to get bitten.
  • Choose softer materials. Plastic, silicone, or flexible bioplast balls and backs are gentler on enamel than metal or glass.
  • Do not play with it. Clicking, rolling, or biting the jewelry is the number one cause of chipped teeth. This one is hard because it becomes a habit you do not notice.
  • Remove it for sports. Contact sports and a mouth full of metal are a bad mix. Wear a sports mouthguard and take the jewelry out.
  • Clean the jewelry daily. Take it out and brush it when you brush your teeth.
  • Check that the balls are tight. A loose ball can be swallowed or inhaled.

For Parents of Teens

Many piercings happen in the teen years. If your teenager is set on getting one, it helps to talk about it before rather than after. Suggest a lip or ear piercing over the tongue, insist on a licensed, reputable piercer, and make sure the next dental checkup includes a look at the area. If your teen already has one, ask us to check for early chips or recession at their cleaning. Catching it early is far easier than fixing a cracked molar later.

What We Look for at Your Checkup

At every exam, we look for small chips along the biting edges, hairline cracks, worn spots on the back of the front teeth, and gum tissue that has started to recede near the jewelry. Let us know you have a piercing, even if you took it out for the visit. If you are scheduled for X-rays, removing the jewelry helps us get a clear picture.

Some patients decide to take the piercing out once they see early damage. That choice is always yours. Our job is to give you the facts and keep your teeth healthy either way.

Common Questions

Can a tongue piercing really crack a tooth?

Yes. A metal barbell clicking against the back of the front teeth or the molars, especially when people play with it or bite down on it, can chip enamel or cause cracks that eventually need a filling, crown, or root canal.

Are plastic or silicone piercing balls safer for teeth?

They are gentler than metal or glass and reduce the risk of chipping, but they do not eliminate it. Shorter barbells and not playing with the jewelry matter just as much as the material.

Should I take my piercing out for a dental appointment?

For routine cleanings and exams you usually do not need to, but remove it for dental X-rays if you can, since metal can block the image. Let our team know ahead of time and we will tell you whether it needs to come out.

Worried About a Chip or Gum Changes?

We will check the area, tell you honestly what we see, and help you protect your smile, piercing or not.
