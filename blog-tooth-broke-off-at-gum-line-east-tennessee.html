Tooth Broke Off at the Gum Line: What to Do and Can It Be Saved?

You bit down, felt a crack, and now there is almost nothing left above the gums. Here is what to do today and what your options really are.

Published November 5, 2026  |  Elite Dental

A tooth that breaks off at the gum line is unsettling. Sometimes it hurts a lot. Sometimes it does not hurt at all, which can be just as confusing. Either way, the questions are the same: Is this an emergency? Can the tooth be saved? What happens next? This guide walks through all three in plain language.

The Short Version

Today: rinse with warm salt water, save any broken piece, avoid chewing on that side, and call us.
Go to the ER instead if swelling is spreading toward your eye or neck, or you have trouble breathing or swallowing.
Can it be saved? Often yes, if enough healthy root is left and the break does not run deep below the bone.
If not: the root is removed and the space can be filled with an implant, bridge, or partial denture.

Why Teeth Break Off at the Gum Line

Healthy teeth rarely snap off without warning. Usually the tooth was already weak. The most common reasons we see are:

  • A large old filling. When most of a tooth is filling material, the thin walls left behind eventually crack.
  • Decay at the gum line. Cavities that start near the gums can hollow out the neck of the tooth until the top simply breaks away.
  • A root canal tooth with no crown. Back teeth that had a root canal become brittle. Without a crown, they are prone to breaking.
  • Grinding and clenching. Years of heavy force, often at night, fatigue the tooth.
  • Trauma. A fall, a sports injury, or biting something hard like an olive pit or a popcorn kernel.

Why It May Not Hurt

Many people are surprised when a tooth breaks and there is little or no pain. That usually means the nerve inside the tooth has already died, often from old decay, or the tooth had a root canal in the past. No pain does not mean no problem. A broken tooth with a dead nerve can still become infected and form an abscess, sometimes weeks or months later.

If it does hurt, especially with sharp pain to air or cold, the nerve may be exposed. That needs attention soon, both for comfort and to keep bacteria out of the root.

What to Do Right Now

  • Rinse gently with warm salt water to clean the area. Our guide to salt water rinses explains how.
  • Save the broken piece if you have it, especially for a front tooth. Keep it in a small container of milk or water and bring it with you.
  • Control bleeding with light pressure from a clean gauze or damp tea bag.
  • Manage pain with over-the-counter pain relievers as directed on the label, if they are safe for you.
  • Chew on the other side and stick to soft foods.
  • Do not try to glue the piece back on with household glue or file down sharp edges yourself.

Then call the office. A tooth broken at the gum line is not always a middle-of-the-night emergency, but it should be seen within a day or two. Our dental emergency guide covers when to call right away.

Can a Tooth Broken at the Gum Line Be Saved?

Often, yes. The deciding factor is how much healthy tooth is left below the break and whether the crack extends deep into the root. We take an x-ray and, when needed, a 3D scan to see exactly where the break ends.

When the tooth can usually be saved

If the root is solid and the break stops at or just below the gum line, the typical path is:

  • Root canal treatment to clean out the nerve space and seal the root. Many teeth broken this low need one, even if they do not hurt.
  • A post and core to rebuild the missing part of the tooth so a crown has something to hold on to.
  • A crown to cover and protect what is left. See our page on crowns and bridges.

For a crown to last, it needs a small collar of real tooth structure to grip, about one and a half to two millimeters above the bone. When the break sits right at the gum, there may not be enough. In that case, a minor gum procedure called crown lengthening can expose more tooth. In some cases, the root can also be gently pulled upward with orthodontics before the crown is placed.

When extraction is the better choice

Sometimes saving the tooth is not realistic or not worth the cost. That is usually the case when:

  • The break runs well below the bone
  • The root itself is cracked vertically
  • Decay has destroyed too much of the remaining root
  • The tooth has advanced gum disease and is loose

Here, we remove the remaining root and plan a replacement. A dental implant is often the most natural and longest lasting option, but a bridge or partial denture can also work well. We cover the tradeoffs in root canal vs. extraction.

Why You Should Not Wait

A broken tooth that does not hurt is easy to put off. The problem is that time usually makes the options worse, not better. The exposed root keeps decaying, which can turn a savable tooth into an extraction. Bacteria can reach the root tip and cause an abscess with swelling and pain. Sharp edges can cut the tongue and cheek. And if a back tooth is left out long enough, neighboring teeth drift and the opposite tooth can grow into the space, which makes any replacement harder.

What If You Are Nervous About Treatment?

A broken tooth is often the moment someone who has avoided the dentist for years finally has to come in. If that is you, you are not alone, and nobody here will lecture you. We offer nitrous oxide, oral, and IV sedation so you can get the tooth fixed comfortably, sometimes in fewer visits.

Common Questions

Is a tooth broken at the gum line an emergency?

It should be seen within a day or two. Call right away if you have severe pain, swelling, or bleeding that will not stop. Go to the emergency room if swelling spreads toward your eye or neck or you have trouble breathing or swallowing.

Can a tooth broken at the gum line be fixed with a crown?

Often, yes. Most teeth broken that low need a root canal and a post and core first, then a crown. If there is not enough tooth above the bone for the crown to grip, crown lengthening can expose more tooth structure.

Why doesn't my broken tooth hurt?

Usually the nerve inside the tooth has already died from old decay or the tooth had a root canal in the past. It still needs to be treated, because a broken tooth with a dead nerve can become infected later.
