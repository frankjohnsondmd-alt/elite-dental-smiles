Tongue Tie in Babies and Kids: Signs, Myths, and When to Treat It

Tongue tie gets a lot of attention online, and parents often leave a visit or a social media scroll more confused than when they started. Here is a plain look at what it is, what it really affects, and when a release is worth talking about.

Published October 29, 2026  |  Elite Dental

Lift your child's tongue and you will see a thin band of tissue connecting the underside of the tongue to the floor of the mouth. That band is called the lingual frenulum, and everyone has one. Tongue tie, or ankyloglossia, is when that band is short, tight, or attached close to the tip, so the tongue cannot move as freely as it should. The key point is simple: tongue tie is about how the tongue works, not how the band looks.

The Short Version

Is it common? A visible frenulum is normal. A true tongue tie that causes problems is much less common.
What matters: function. Can your child feed, eat, and speak without trouble?
Does it always need treatment? No. Many kids with a short frenulum never have a single issue.
When to get it checked: ongoing feeding trouble, poor weight gain, or tongue movement problems that affect eating or speech.

What Tongue Tie Looks Like

Some common physical signs include:

  • The tongue tip looks notched or heart shaped when your child sticks it out.
  • The tongue cannot reach past the lower lip.
  • Your child has trouble lifting the tongue to the roof of the mouth.
  • The tongue cannot move side to side easily to lick the corners of the lips.

Here is the catch. Plenty of children have one or more of these features and eat, talk, and grow perfectly well. Appearance alone is not a reason to treat.

Signs in Babies

In infants, tongue tie mostly shows up as a feeding problem, especially with breastfeeding. Signs that are worth a closer look include:

  • Trouble latching or staying latched.
  • Clicking sounds while nursing or bottle feeding.
  • Very long or very frequent feeds that never seem to satisfy the baby.
  • Poor weight gain.
  • Nipple pain, cracking, or damage for the nursing parent that does not improve with positioning help.

Every one of these can also have causes that have nothing to do with the tongue. The best first step is usually a visit with your pediatrician and a lactation consultant. Many feeding problems improve with technique changes alone, and if they do not, the tongue becomes a more likely suspect.

Signs in Older Kids

For toddlers and school-age children, the questions shift to eating, speaking, and keeping the mouth clean:

  • Speech: some children have trouble with sounds that need the tongue tip to lift, such as "t," "d," "l," and "n." Most speech delays are not caused by tongue tie, though, so a speech-language pathologist is the right person to sort that out.
  • Eating: trouble moving food around the mouth, gagging on certain textures, or very slow eating.
  • Oral hygiene: a tongue that cannot sweep the teeth leaves more food behind, especially along the back teeth and gumline, which can raise cavity risk.

What About Lip Tie?

You may also hear about lip tie, which is the band of tissue between the upper lip and the gums above the front teeth. In babies and young children, this band is often thick and attached low. That is normal, and it usually thins and moves up as the face grows. A space between the upper front teeth is common in young kids and often closes as adult teeth come in (see our article on a gap between the front teeth). Lip tie alone rarely needs treatment in infancy.

A Word of Caution About Over-Treatment

Tongue tie releases have become far more common over the last two decades. The American Academy of Pediatrics has noted that the number of procedures has climbed faster than the evidence supporting them, and that some babies are treated for problems the tongue is not actually causing. A release is a real procedure on a very small patient, so it should solve a real, specific problem. If someone recommends one, it is fair to ask what exactly it is expected to fix, and what else has been tried.

When a Release Makes Sense

When a tight frenulum clearly limits tongue movement and is causing a problem that has not improved with other help, a release can make a meaningful difference. The procedure, called a frenotomy or frenectomy, frees the band so the tongue can move normally. After a release, follow-up matters. Feeding support for babies, or exercises and speech therapy for older kids, is often what turns a technically successful procedure into a real improvement.

The Bottom Line

A visible frenulum is normal, and a short one does not automatically mean trouble. What matters is whether your child can feed, eat, speak, and keep their teeth clean without struggling. If they cannot, and other help has not solved it, a careful evaluation is worth your time.

Common Questions

Does every tongue tie need to be clipped?

No. Many children with a short or tight frenulum feed, eat, and speak normally and never need treatment. A release is worth considering only when the tongue's limited movement is causing a specific problem that has not improved with other help.

Can tongue tie cause speech problems?

It can affect some sounds that require lifting the tongue tip, but most speech delays are not caused by tongue tie. A speech-language pathologist can tell whether the tongue is the issue or something else is going on.

Will a lip tie cause a gap between my child's front teeth?

A low, thick upper lip attachment is normal in young children and usually changes as the face grows. Gaps between baby teeth are common and often close as adult teeth come in. Treatment is rarely needed in infancy.
