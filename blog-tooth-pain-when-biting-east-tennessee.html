Why Does My Tooth Hurt When I Bite?

Pain under pressure is your tooth's warning signal. Here is what it may mean and what to do next.

Published August 27, 2026 | Elite Dental

A tooth can feel normal until you chew something firm, then suddenly send a sharp jolt through your jaw. Pain when biting is easy to dismiss because it may disappear as soon as you release pressure. Still, recurring pain usually means something has changed in the tooth, filling, supporting bone, or surrounding ligament.

The cause cannot be diagnosed by sensation alone. A dental exam and X-rays may be needed, and very small cracks do not always appear clearly on an X-ray. The pattern of the pain, the condition of existing dental work, and how the tooth responds to temperature and pressure all provide useful clues.

Common Causes of Pain When Chewing

A cracked toothA crack can flex under chewing pressure and irritate the nerve inside.
A loose filling or crownDental work that no longer seals well can move, leak, or expose sensitive tooth structure.
A bite that is too highA recent filling or crown may contact first and overload the ligament around the tooth.
Decay or infectionA deep cavity or infection near the root can make pressure painful.

Gum disease can also make a tooth tender when chewing, especially if supporting bone has been lost or food repeatedly becomes trapped beside it. Occasionally, sinus pressure causes upper back teeth to feel sore, but sinus discomfort commonly affects several teeth rather than one precise spot.

Does It Hurt When You Bite Down or Let Go?

This distinction can help your dentist. Pain as you bite down may point to inflammation, an uneven bite, decay, or infection. A sharp pain when you release your bite can be associated with a cracked cusp or cracked tooth. Neither pattern proves a diagnosis, but both deserve attention if they repeat.

Cracks are especially unpredictable. Some remain limited to the outer part of a tooth and can be protected with a crown. Others extend toward the nerve and may require root canal treatment. A crack that travels deeply below the gumline may make the tooth impossible to save. Earlier evaluation usually preserves more choices.

Do Not Keep Testing the Tooth

Repeatedly biting on hard foods to see whether the pain is still present can make a crack worse. Chew on the other side, avoid ice and hard candy, and arrange an exam.

What You Can Do Before Your Appointment

Keep the area clean with gentle brushing and flossing. If food is caught between the teeth, carefully remove it. Choose softer foods and avoid extreme temperatures if they trigger discomfort. Over-the-counter pain medicine may help when it is safe for you, but do not place aspirin directly against the tooth or gum because it can burn the tissue.

Make a note of what triggers the pain. Tell your dentist whether it hurts with pressure, release, cold, heat, or sweets, and whether discomfort lingers afterward. Mention any recent filling, crown, injury, teeth grinding, or habit of chewing ice. These details can shorten the path to the right diagnosis.

When Tooth Pain Needs Prompt Care

Schedule an evaluation if the pain repeats, worsens, or lasts more than a day or two. Call sooner if you develop swelling, fever, a bad taste, drainage, severe throbbing, or pain that wakes you at night. Facial swelling, difficulty swallowing, or difficulty breathing requires urgent medical attention.

Treatment depends on the cause. A high filling may need a small adjustment. A damaged restoration may need replacement. A crack may need a crown, while an inflamed or infected nerve may require root canal treatment. If gum disease or trapped food is responsible, cleaning and a plan to correct the underlying problem may provide relief.

Common Questions

Can pain when biting go away on its own?

Minor ligament irritation sometimes settles, but persistent or recurring pain should not be ignored. Cracks, decay, and infection usually do not repair themselves.

Is pain after a new filling normal?

Brief sensitivity can occur, but the tooth should be checked if it feels taller than the others, hurts each time you chew, or is not improving.

Will I need a root canal?

Not necessarily. The right treatment depends on the depth of damage and whether the nerve is healthy, inflamed, or infected.
