My Child Grinds Their Teeth at Night: Should I Worry?

That grinding sound down the hall is unsettling, but it is also very common in children. Here is how to tell the harmless kind from the kind worth treating.

Published October 4, 2026  |  Elite Dental

Plenty of parents in Dandridge and Jefferson City hear it for the first time while checking on a sleeping child. It is a slow, gritty scraping sound, loud enough to be alarming. The clinical name is bruxism, and in children it is far more common than most families expect.

Estimates vary, but a large share of kids grind or clench at some point, most often between ages 3 and 10. The majority outgrow it. Baby teeth fall out, permanent teeth come in, the bite settles, and the habit fades without any treatment at all. So the real question is not whether your child grinds. It is whether the grinding is causing damage, pain, or disrupted sleep.

Why Children Grind Their Teeth

There is rarely one single cause. In young children, grinding is often tied to the simple mechanics of a mouth in transition. New teeth erupt at different heights, so the bite is uneven for months at a time, and the jaw muscles explore that unevenness during sleep.

Beyond that, the usual contributors are stress and routine disruption, airway and breathing issues, and occasionally medications or developmental conditions. A child starting a new school, adjusting to a new sibling, or going through a rough stretch at home may grind more. So may a child who breathes through their mouth, snores, or has enlarged tonsils and adenoids.

Changing BiteErupting and shedding teeth make the bite uneven for long stretches of childhood.
Stress or AnxietySchedule changes, new schools, and family stress can show up as nighttime clenching.
Airway and BreathingMouth breathing, snoring, or large tonsils can accompany grinding during sleep.
Habit and Muscle PatternSome children simply hold tension in the jaw, especially during deeper sleep stages.

When Grinding Is Probably Fine

If your child grinds occasionally, wakes without complaints, eats normally, and their teeth look intact at checkups, monitoring is usually all that is needed. Mention it at every visit so we can compare the biting surfaces over time. A photo or two in the chart makes it much easier to tell real wear from normal variation a year later.

Signs That Deserve a Dental Visit

Call us sooner if you notice flattened or shortened teeth, chipped enamel, visible notches on the biting surfaces, or teeth that look shorter than they did last year. The same goes for complaints of jaw soreness or headaches in the morning, sensitivity to cold, pain while chewing, or clicking and popping in the jaw joint.

Breathing and sleep symptoms matter just as much. Loud snoring, pauses in breathing, chronic mouth breathing, night sweats, bedwetting that restarts, or daytime fatigue and irritability are worth raising. Grinding that travels with those symptoms points toward an airway evaluation rather than a dental appliance.

Most Children Do Not Need a Night Guard

A growing mouth changes shape quickly, so a custom guard made this spring may not fit by fall. Guards are reserved for older children with real wear on permanent teeth, ongoing pain, or damage we can measure. For most kids, the right answer is observation plus addressing whatever is driving the grinding.

What We Look At During an Exam

We check the wear pattern on both baby and permanent teeth, how the upper and lower teeth meet, the jaw joints and chewing muscles, and the size of the tonsils and the shape of the palate. We also ask about sleep: how your child breathes at night, how restless they are, and whether anyone else in the family grinds.

That full picture is what determines the plan. Mild wear with good sleep and no symptoms gets watched. Wear plus snoring gets a conversation about airway and a possible referral. Significant wear on permanent teeth with jaw pain is where a protective appliance starts to make sense.

What Parents Can Do Tonight

Start with sleep quality, since it is the lever you control. A consistent bedtime, a wind-down period without screens, and a cool dark room all help. Cut caffeine, including sodas and chocolate, in the afternoon and evening.

If stress is a factor, give it somewhere to go before bed. Reading together, talking through the day, a warm bath, or a short stretching routine can lower muscle tension. Avoid making the grinding itself a source of worry for your child, because a kid who feels monitored and judged at bedtime tends to sleep worse, not better.

Keep a simple log for two weeks. Note which nights the grinding is loud, what happened that day, and how your child feels in the morning. Patterns show up fast, and that log is genuinely useful information at the next appointment.

Common Questions

Is it normal for a child to grind their teeth at night?

Yes. It is common between ages 3 and 10, and most children outgrow it as their baby teeth are replaced and the bite settles.

Will grinding damage my child's teeth?

Most children grind without lasting harm. Heavy, long-term grinding can flatten biting surfaces, chip enamel, or cause sensitivity, which is why tracking the wear over time matters.

Does my child need a night guard?

Usually not. Guards are considered for older children with measurable wear on permanent teeth, jaw pain, or damage, because a growing mouth outgrows an appliance quickly.

Could grinding mean a sleep or breathing problem?

It can. Grinding alongside snoring, mouth breathing, restless sleep, or daytime fatigue is worth evaluating, since treating the airway sometimes resolves the grinding.
