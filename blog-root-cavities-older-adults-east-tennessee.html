Root Cavities: Why Older Adults Get Cavities Near the Gumline

You went decades without a cavity, and now your dentist is finding them along the gumline. Here is why that happens and how to stop it.

Published November 9, 2026  |  Elite Dental

One of the most common surprises we see in our Dandridge and Jefferson City offices is an adult in their fifties, sixties, or seventies who has not had a cavity in years, suddenly needing several fillings at once. Almost always, those cavities are in the same place: right at the gumline, on the root surface of the tooth. These are called root cavities, and they are one of the biggest dental problems facing older adults in East Tennessee.

Root cavities are not a sign that you stopped taking care of your teeth. They happen because your mouth changes with age, and the part of the tooth that is now exposed was never built to resist decay the way enamel does.

The Short Version

What they are: cavities on the root surface of a tooth, usually where the gums have pulled back.
Why they happen: exposed roots are softer than enamel, and dry mouth from medications removes your natural protection.
Why they matter: root decay spreads fast and can wrap around a tooth before it hurts.
How to prevent them: prescription fluoride, careful cleaning at the gumline, managing dry mouth, and regular checkups.

What Makes a Root Cavity Different

The crown of your tooth, the part you normally see, is covered in enamel. Enamel is the hardest substance in the body. Below the gumline, the root is covered by a thin layer called cementum, with softer dentin underneath. Neither one is nearly as tough as enamel.

When your gums are healthy and sit at their normal height, the root is protected. But when gums recede, that softer root surface is exposed to the same bacteria, acids, and sugars that attack the rest of your teeth. Root surfaces begin to break down at a less acidic level than enamel does, which means decay can start sooner and move faster.

Root cavities also tend to spread sideways along the gumline rather than straight down. In advanced cases, decay can wrap around the neck of the tooth and weaken it enough that the tooth breaks off at the gum. Our article on a tooth that broke off at the gumline explains what happens next if that occurs.

Why Older Adults Are at Higher Risk

Gum recession

Gums naturally recede a little over a lifetime, and gum disease, aggressive brushing, and grinding can speed that up. Every millimeter of recession exposes more root surface. If you have noticed your teeth looking longer, read our guide to gum recession.

Dry mouth from medications

This is the biggest driver we see. Hundreds of common medications reduce saliva, including drugs for blood pressure, depression, anxiety, allergies, bladder control, and pain. Saliva rinses away food, neutralizes acid, and delivers minerals that repair early damage. When saliva drops, root surfaces lose their main defense. Our article on medications that cause dry mouth lists the most common ones.

Changes in diet and snacking

Retirement often changes daily routines. Sipping sweet tea or coffee with sugar throughout the day, sucking on hard candies or cough drops for a dry throat, or snacking more often all keep teeth bathed in sugar for longer periods. Frequency matters more than quantity.

Harder to clean at home

Arthritis, reduced vision, and older crowns or bridges all make it tougher to clean right at the gumline. If your hands are part of the problem, our article on brushing and flossing with arthritis has practical tips.

Older dental work

The edges of old fillings and crowns often sit near the gumline. As gums recede, those margins become an easy place for new decay to start.

Signs You Might Have a Root Cavity

Early root cavities rarely hurt, which is why regular exams matter so much. When symptoms do appear, they can include:

  • A brown, yellow, or dark spot at the gumline that was not there before
  • Sensitivity to cold, sweets, or brushing near the gums
  • A rough or soft area your tongue or fingernail catches on
  • Food catching in one spot near the gum

Keep in mind that tooth sensitivity near the gumline can also come from exposed roots without decay, or from worn notches caused by hard brushing. A dental exam is the only way to know which one you are dealing with.

How We Treat Root Cavities

Treatment depends on how early we catch it. Very early root decay that has not formed a hole can sometimes be stopped with high-strength fluoride varnish and better home care, and we monitor it closely. Silver diamine fluoride is another option that can halt active decay on root surfaces, though it stains the treated area dark, so it is usually reserved for spots that do not show when you smile.

Once a cavity has formed, we remove the decay and place a tooth-colored filling. Fillings at the gumline can be tricky because the area is hard to keep dry, so careful technique matters. If decay has spread around much of the tooth, a crown may be needed, and teeth with very deep decay may need a root canal. Catching it early keeps treatment small and affordable.

How to Prevent Root Cavities

  • Use prescription-strength fluoride toothpaste. For adults with exposed roots or dry mouth, this is one of the most effective things you can do. Spit after brushing, but do not rinse, so the fluoride stays on your teeth.
  • Get fluoride varnish at cleanings. It is quick, painless, and strengthens root surfaces between visits.
  • Clean the gumline gently. Use a soft brush or an electric brush angled toward the gums, and clean between teeth daily with floss, picks, or a water flosser.
  • Fight dry mouth. Sip water through the day, use sugar-free lozenges or xylitol gum, and ask your physician whether any medication can be adjusted.
  • Cut down on sipping and grazing. Enjoy sweet drinks with meals instead of all afternoon, and choose sugar-free cough drops.
  • Keep regular visits. Many older adults do best with cleanings every three or four months. See our guide to dental care for seniors for more.

Common Questions

What is a root cavity?

A root cavity is tooth decay on the root surface of a tooth, usually at or just below the gumline where the gums have receded. Root surfaces are softer than enamel, so they decay more easily and faster.

Why am I getting cavities at the gumline as I get older?

The most common reasons are gum recession, which exposes softer root surfaces, and dry mouth caused by medications. Changes in diet, harder cleaning due to arthritis, and older dental work near the gums also raise the risk.

Can root cavities be prevented?

Yes. Prescription-strength fluoride toothpaste, fluoride varnish at cleanings, careful cleaning along the gumline, managing dry mouth, and regular dental checkups prevent most root cavities or catch them while they are still small.

Catch Root Decay Early

If your gums have receded or you take medications that dry your mouth, a checkup can find root cavities before they become a bigger problem.
