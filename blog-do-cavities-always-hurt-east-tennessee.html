Do Cavities Always Hurt? Why Most Cavities Are Silent Until They Are Big

"Nothing hurts, so my teeth must be fine." It is one of the most common things we hear, and one of the most expensive assumptions in dentistry.

Published November 2, 2026  |  Elite Dental

Most people expect a cavity to announce itself with a toothache. In reality, the large majority of cavities cause no pain at all for months or even years. By the time a tooth hurts, the decay is often deep, and the simple filling it once needed may have turned into a crown, a root canal, or an extraction. Here is why cavities stay quiet, the small clues worth paying attention to, and how to catch decay while it is still easy to fix.

The Short Version

Do cavities always hurt? No. Most early cavities cause no pain at all.
Why: enamel, the outer layer of the tooth, has no nerves.
When pain starts: usually once decay reaches the deeper dentin or gets close to the nerve.
Best defense: regular exams and x-rays, which find decay before you can feel it.

Why Early Cavities Do Not Hurt

A tooth is built in layers. On the outside is enamel, the hardest substance in the body. Enamel has no nerves and no blood supply, so decay can eat into it without sending any signal at all. Under the enamel is dentin, a softer layer filled with tiny tubes that lead toward the nerve. At the center is the pulp, where the nerve and blood vessels live.

A cavity starts in the enamel and works its way inward. While it stays in the enamel, you feel nothing. Once it reaches the dentin, it spreads faster, because dentin is softer, and it may start to cause mild sensitivity. Real pain usually shows up only when the decay is close to the pulp or has already infected it. In other words, the toothache comes at the end of the story, not the beginning.

Where Silent Cavities Hide

Some of the most common cavities are also the hardest to notice on your own:

  • Between the back teeth. Decay often starts where two teeth touch. You cannot see it in a mirror, and it can grow large before the tooth hurts. These are usually found on bite-wing x-rays.
  • Under old fillings and crowns. Decay can creep in along the edges of older work. Our article on recurring cavities explains why some people keep getting them.
  • Along the gumline. Exposed roots are softer than enamel and decay quickly, especially in people with dry mouth or receding gums.
  • In the grooves of the molars. Deep pits trap food and bacteria, and decay can spread underneath while the surface still looks fine.

Subtle Signs You Might Have a Cavity

Even without pain, a cavity sometimes gives small hints. None of these prove you have one, but they are worth mentioning at your next visit:

  • A quick zing with cold drinks or ice cream that fades right away. See our guide to a tooth sensitive to cold.
  • A twinge when eating something sweet.
  • Floss that catches or shreds in the same spot every time.
  • Food packing between two teeth where it never used to.
  • A rough edge, a small hole, or a spot you can feel with your tongue.
  • A white, brown, or dark spot that was not there before.
  • Bad breath or a bad taste that keeps coming back from one area.

When a Cavity Does Hurt, It Is Usually Further Along

Pain changes the picture. Sensitivity that lingers for more than a few seconds after something hot or cold, a tooth that throbs on its own, pain that wakes you up, or pain when you bite down are all signs that decay may be reaching the nerve. At that point, a filling may no longer be enough. The tooth may need a crown, a root canal, or in some cases removal.

Be careful with one situation in particular. If a tooth that hurt badly suddenly stops hurting, do not take that as good news. It can mean the nerve has died, and a dead nerve often turns into an abscess. Swelling, a pimple on the gum, or a bad taste are signs to call right away.

Why Waiting for Pain Costs More

A small cavity caught early is usually a quick, tooth-colored filling, often done in one short visit. The same cavity left alone for a year or two can become a much bigger job. Here is the typical progression:

  • Early enamel decay: sometimes stopped with fluoride and better habits, no drilling needed.
  • Small cavity into dentin: a simple filling.
  • Large cavity: a bigger filling, an onlay, or a crown.
  • Decay into the nerve: a root canal and crown, or an extraction and replacement.

Each step up costs more time, more money, and more of your natural tooth. It is also why we answer the question can a cavity heal on its own the way we do: only the earliest enamel spots can be reversed. Once there is an actual hole, it will not fill itself back in.

How We Find Cavities Before You Feel Them

At a routine checkup, we look at every surface of every tooth and take bite-wing x-rays at the right intervals for your risk level. Our article on how often you need dental x-rays covers that in more detail. Those x-rays are what catch the decay hiding between teeth, which is exactly the kind that never hurts until it is large.

If you are prone to cavities, we may also recommend prescription toothpaste, fluoride varnish, or more frequent visits to keep small spots from turning into big ones.

Common Questions

Can I have a cavity and not feel any pain?

Yes, and that is the most common situation. Enamel has no nerves, so a cavity usually causes no pain until it reaches the dentin underneath or gets close to the nerve. Many cavities are found on x-rays or during an exam before the patient feels anything.

If my tooth stopped hurting, is the cavity gone?

Usually not. A cavity cannot fill itself back in. When a tooth that hurt suddenly stops hurting, it can mean the nerve has died, which often leads to an abscess. A tooth that went quiet still needs to be checked.

How can a dentist find a cavity that does not hurt?

Through a careful exam and bite-wing x-rays, which show decay between the back teeth where you cannot see it in the mirror. That is the main reason routine checkups find problems while they are still small and inexpensive to fix.
