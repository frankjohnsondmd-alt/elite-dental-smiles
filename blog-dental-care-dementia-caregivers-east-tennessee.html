Dental Care for a Loved One With Dementia: A Caregiver Guide

Brushing someone else's teeth is harder than it sounds, especially when they do not understand why. Here is how to protect their comfort and health without turning every night into a struggle.

Published November 14, 2026  |  Elite Dental

November is National Alzheimer's Disease Awareness Month, and it is a good time to talk about something that often gets lost in the daily work of caregiving: the mouth. When you are managing medications, meals, appointments, and safety for a parent or spouse living with dementia, brushing their teeth can easily slide to the bottom of the list.

We understand why. But in our Dandridge and Jefferson City offices, we regularly see how quickly dental problems build up once a person can no longer care for their own teeth. The good news is that a few simple habits can prevent most of the pain, infections, and emergency visits that come with it.

The Short Version

Brush twice a day with a fluoride toothpaste, even if it is only a quick pass.
Remove and clean dentures every night.
Watch behavior, not words, for signs of pain.
See the dentist every three to six months, and earlier in the disease is better.

Why Dental Health Slips With Dementia

Brushing and flossing are multi-step tasks. As memory and planning skills decline, a person may forget whether they brushed, forget how, or simply stop seeing the point. Many people also take medications that cause dry mouth, and saliva is one of the mouth's main defenses against decay.

Diet often changes too. Sweet drinks, nutrition shakes, and soft snacks are common, and they tend to sit on the teeth for long periods. Put those factors together and older adults with dementia are at high risk for root cavities and gum disease, sometimes developing several new problems within a single year.

Pain Often Shows Up as Behavior

One of the hardest parts of dental care in dementia is that the person may not be able to tell you something hurts. Instead, pain tends to come out sideways. Watch for:

  • Refusing to eat, or only eating soft or cold foods
  • Chewing on one side or holding food in the mouth
  • Pulling at the face, cheek, or jaw
  • New agitation, restlessness, or trouble sleeping
  • Refusing to wear dentures they used to wear without complaint
  • Swelling of the face or gums, or a bad smell from the mouth

If you notice any of these, it is worth having the mouth checked before assuming it is just the disease progressing. We have seen patients whose "bad days" improved significantly once an infected tooth was treated.

Making Daily Brushing Easier

There is no perfect technique, but these approaches help many families:

  • Keep the same time and place every day. Routine reduces resistance.
  • Let them do what they can. Hand them the brush and guide their hand, then finish the spots they miss.
  • Use short, simple cues. "Open." "Brush top teeth." "Spit." One step at a time.
  • Approach from the side or behind. Standing directly in front can feel confrontational.
  • Try a different brush. A soft brush with a thick handle is easier to grip. An electric toothbrush does more of the work, though some people dislike the vibration.
  • Use only a pea-sized amount of toothpaste. If they tend to swallow it, a non-foaming toothpaste can help.
  • If they refuse, stop. Try again in 15 minutes. Forcing it usually makes the next attempt harder.

Flossing may become impractical. If so, floss picks or small interdental brushes are a reasonable substitute. Ask us about a prescription-strength fluoride toothpaste, which can make a real difference for people at high risk of decay.

Caring for Dentures

Dentures need just as much attention as natural teeth. Take them out every night, brush them, and soak them in water or a denture cleaner. Leaving dentures in around the clock can cause sore spots, fungal infections, and swelling under the plate. If the person has new denture sore spots or will not keep their dentures in, the fit may have changed with weight loss and should be checked.

It also helps to label dentures with the person's name, especially if they live in assisted living or spend time in a hospital. Lost dentures are a surprisingly common problem.

Plan Dental Treatment Early

If your loved one was recently diagnosed, the best time for a thorough dental exam is now. In the early stages, most people can still sit comfortably for cleanings, fillings, and longer appointments. As the disease progresses, treatment becomes harder to complete and may require sedation.

A good dentist will help you prioritize. Sometimes that means fixing problems now that would be difficult to manage later. Other times it means choosing simpler, lower-maintenance options rather than complex treatment. The goal is a mouth that stays comfortable and easy to keep clean for the years ahead.

What to Expect at the Dental Office

Bring a list of current medications and any medical history you can. Let our team know ahead of time about anything that tends to upset your loved one, such as bright lights, waiting rooms, or being touched without warning. Morning appointments often go better because many people with dementia are more settled earlier in the day.

You are welcome to stay in the room, and your familiar voice can be very calming. For patients who cannot tolerate treatment while awake, we can discuss sedation options, including IV sedation, along with any medical clearance that may be needed.

Take Care of Yourself, Too

Caregiving is exhausting, and oral care can feel like one more battle in a long day. Do what you can. Brushing once well is better than skipping entirely, and a regular dental visit catches most problems before they become emergencies. Common Questions

How often should someone with dementia see the dentist?

Most people living with dementia should keep a cleaning and exam schedule of every three to six months. More frequent visits help catch problems early, since many patients cannot clearly describe pain.

What if my parent refuses to let me brush their teeth?

Stop and try again later rather than forcing it. Changing the time of day, standing behind or beside them, using a calm step-by-step routine, or handing them the brush to start can all help. Even brushing part of the mouth is better than none.

How can I tell if someone with dementia has tooth pain?

Watch for changes in behavior: refusing food, chewing on one side, pulling at the face or mouth, new agitation, trouble sleeping, or pushing dentures away. These can all be signs of a dental problem.
