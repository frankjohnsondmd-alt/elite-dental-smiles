Why Does My Tooth Hurt When I Bite Down?

Pain from chewing is often a clue that a tooth, restoration, nerve, or supporting tissue needs attention.

Published September 12, 2026 | Elite Dental

A tooth that hurts only when you bite can be confusing. It may feel normal at rest and react sharply when you chew something firm or release pressure. That pattern matters. Bite pain often points to a mechanical problem, inflammation around the root, or damage that is difficult to see without a dental exam.

The pain may be mild and temporary, but it should not be ignored if it persists or returns. Teeth cannot heal cracks or decay on their own, and an early evaluation may make treatment simpler. A dentist can reproduce the symptom safely, isolate the responsible tooth, and determine whether the problem involves the tooth itself or the tissue around it.

Common Reasons a Tooth Hurts When Chewing

A cracked tooth

Small cracks can flex as you bite. Some cause pain under pressure, while others hurt most when you release the bite. The discomfort may come and go depending on where food contacts the tooth. Cracks are not always visible on an X-ray, so dentists also use bite tests, magnification, light, and a careful clinical exam.

A filling or crown that is too high

After dental work, a restoration may contact the opposing tooth slightly before the rest of your bite. That extra force can inflame the ligament around the root and make the tooth feel bruised. A small bite adjustment may solve the problem, but the tooth should be checked rather than avoided for weeks.

Decay or a damaged restoration

A cavity can weaken tooth structure and allow pressure, temperature, and bacteria to reach sensitive areas. An old filling may also chip, loosen, or lose its seal. Pain when biting can be the first noticeable symptom even when the tooth looks normal in the mirror.

Nerve inflammation or infection

Deep decay, trauma, or a crack can inflame the pulp inside a tooth. As inflammation progresses, the tooth may become sensitive to cold or heat, ache without stimulation, or hurt when tapped. If infection reaches the root area, biting pressure can become especially uncomfortable.

Gum or ligament irritation

Food trapped below the gumline, gum disease, clenching, grinding, or a recent impact can irritate the structures supporting a tooth. The tooth may feel taller than the others or tender when chewing. The right treatment depends on whether the source is bacterial, bite-related, or traumatic.

When to Seek Prompt Care

Call a dentist soon if the pain lasts more than a day or two, keeps returning, prevents normal chewing, or follows recent dental work. Seek urgent care for facial swelling, fever, drainage, severe spontaneous pain, a broken tooth, or difficulty swallowing or breathing.

What to Do Until Your Appointment

Chew on the opposite side and choose softer foods. Brush gently and floss to remove trapped debris. If pain medicine is safe for you, use it only as directed on the label. Do not place aspirin on the tooth or gum because it can burn the tissue, and do not keep testing the painful tooth with hard foods.

Avoid assuming that the absence of constant pain means the problem is minor. A cracked tooth or failing restoration can remain quiet between meals, then worsen suddenly. Temporary improvement does not confirm that the source has healed.

How Elite Dental Diagnoses Bite Pain

We ask what triggers the pain, how long it lasts, and whether the tooth reacts to temperature or release of pressure. The examination may include bite-paper checks, individual cusp testing, cold testing, tapping, gum measurements, and dental X-rays. These findings help distinguish a high restoration from a crack, cavity, nerve problem, or gum issue.

Treatment might be a simple bite adjustment, a new filling, a crown to protect weakened tooth structure, gum treatment, root canal therapy, or extraction when a tooth cannot be predictably saved. The goal is to identify the cause early and preserve as much healthy tooth as possible.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why does only one tooth hurt when I bite?

A localized problem such as a crack, cavity, high filling, infection, or irritated ligament commonly affects one tooth. An exam is the best way to tell them apart.

Can a cracked tooth stop hurting?

Symptoms can come and go, but the crack does not repair itself. Earlier diagnosis may improve the chance of protecting the tooth.

Should I wait if the pain is mild?

If the discomfort returns with chewing or lasts beyond a day or two, schedule an evaluation. Prompt care can prevent a small problem from becoming an emergency.
