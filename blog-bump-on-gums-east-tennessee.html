Why Is There a Bump on My Gums?

A gum bump may be harmless irritation, but it can also signal an infection that needs dental care.

Published September 26, 2026  |  Elite Dental

Finding a bump on your gums can be unsettling, especially when it appears suddenly or sits beside a tooth that has bothered you before. Gum bumps have several possible causes. Some are minor and resolve after an irritant is removed. Others are drainage points for an infection beneath the gum and should be examined promptly.

Appearance alone rarely gives the full answer. A dentist considers the bump's location, color, tenderness, duration, and relationship to nearby teeth. Dental X-rays and simple testing may be needed to identify where it started. Two bumps that look similar can require completely different treatment.

Take note of when you first saw the area, whether it changes after meals, and whether a nearby tooth has had a large filling, crown, injury, or root canal. Those details can help narrow the diagnosis.

A Dental Abscess or Gum Boil

A small pimple-like bump beside a tooth may be a fistula, sometimes called a gum boil. It can form when an infection around a tooth's root creates a pathway for drainage. The bump may release fluid, leave a bad taste, shrink, and later return. Because pressure can escape through the opening, the tooth may not be very painful.

That lack of pain does not mean the infection has healed. Bacteria can remain around the root and damage nearby bone. Treatment may involve root canal therapy, periodontal care, or removal of a tooth that cannot be restored. Antibiotics alone usually do not eliminate the source of a chronic dental infection.

An Abscess in the Gum Tissue

Not every abscess begins inside a tooth. Food, tartar, or bacteria can become trapped in a deep gum pocket and cause a periodontal abscess. This may create a tender swelling along the side of a tooth, often with redness, bleeding, or pain when chewing. The tooth may feel loose or taller than surrounding teeth.

A dentist can measure the gum pocket, evaluate the tooth's nerve, and study an X-ray to distinguish a gum infection from an infection at the root. Treatment focuses on drainage, cleaning the pocket, and controlling underlying gum disease.

When to Seek Urgent Care

Call a dentist promptly if a gum bump comes with facial swelling, fever, worsening pain, difficulty swallowing, trouble breathing, or swelling near the eye. Breathing or swallowing difficulty is an emergency. Do not squeeze or puncture the bump, since that can irritate tissue without treating the source.

Irritation, Canker Sores, and Injury

A sharp chip, toothbrush injury, hard food, or poorly fitting appliance can irritate the gums. A canker sore may begin as a tender raised area before developing a shallow white or yellow center with a red border. These sores are not contagious and usually improve within one to two weeks.

Remove obvious sources of irritation when it is safe to do so, brush gently, and rinse with warm salt water. Avoid picking the area or placing aspirin directly on it, which can burn tissue. A sore that lasts longer than two weeks, grows, repeatedly returns in the same place, or feels firm should be checked.

Normal Bone and Benign Growths

Some firm bumps are normal variations. Tori and exostoses are harmless areas of extra bone that often feel hard, smooth, and painless. They may have been present for years even if you only recently noticed them. Treatment is usually unnecessary unless they interfere with eating, speech, a denture, or another dental procedure.

Other benign growths can develop after repeated irritation. A dentist may monitor a small stable area or recommend removal and laboratory evaluation when the diagnosis is uncertain. Any new growth deserves attention if it changes color, bleeds easily, becomes numb, develops an ulcer, or does not heal.

Could It Be an Erupting Tooth?

In children, a bluish or clear bump may form over a tooth that is about to erupt. Wisdom teeth can also create swelling or a flap of irritated gum at the back of the mouth. Food and bacteria may collect beneath that flap, causing tenderness, a bad taste, or difficulty opening the mouth.

An exam can confirm whether a tooth is erupting normally or lacks enough room. Keeping the area clean may settle mild irritation, while persistent infection around a wisdom tooth may require more definitive care.

What Happens at the Dental Visit?

Your dentist will ask when the bump appeared and whether it drains, changes size, or causes pain. Nearby teeth may be tested for sensitivity, cracks, looseness, and tenderness. Gum measurements and X-rays help locate infection or bone changes. If the area looks unusual, a biopsy or specialist referral may be advised.

Until your visit, continue gentle brushing and flossing unless the area is too painful. Warm salt water can soothe irritated tissue. Avoid tobacco and do not delay care because a bump has temporarily disappeared. A recurring gum bump is often the body's warning that the underlying cause is still present.
