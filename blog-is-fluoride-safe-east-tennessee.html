Is Fluoride Safe? What East Tennessee Families Should Know

Fluoride has been in the news a lot, and parents are asking us whether it is still a good idea. It is a fair question. Here is a straightforward look at what fluoride does, what the research actually says, and how to use it wisely for your family.

Published October 24, 2026  |  Elite Dental

At our Dandridge and Jefferson City offices, we hear two very different worries about fluoride. Some parents are afraid it is harmful. Others are surprised to learn their child's cavities might have been prevented with it. Both concerns deserve a clear answer, not a slogan. The honest summary is that the dose matters, and at the amounts used in toothpaste and community water, fluoride remains one of the most effective and well studied tools we have for preventing tooth decay.

The Short Version

What it does: strengthens enamel and helps repair the earliest stage of cavities.
Recommended water level: 0.7 milligrams per liter in the United States.
Where the concern is: exposure well above that level, mostly from naturally high fluoride in some water supplies.
The practical rule: brush with the right amount of fluoride toothpaste, spit instead of swallowing, and know what is in your water.

How Fluoride Protects Teeth

Every time you eat, bacteria in plaque produce acid that pulls minerals out of your enamel. Saliva puts some of those minerals back between meals. Fluoride tips that balance in your favor. It helps enamel rebuild with a harder, more acid resistant surface, and it slows the bacteria that cause decay.

This works mostly at the surface of the tooth, which is why brushing with fluoride toothpaste is so effective. It is also why an early white spot on a tooth can sometimes harden back up instead of turning into a hole. Our article on whether a cavity can heal on its own explains where that line is.

What the Research Says About Safety

Like almost anything, fluoride can cause problems in high enough amounts. Vitamins, iron, and even water work the same way. The question is not whether fluoride is safe in any amount, but whether it is safe at the amounts people actually use.

In 2024, a federal National Toxicology Program review concluded with moderate confidence that fluoride exposure above 1.5 milligrams per liter, more than twice the recommended level for community water, is associated with lower IQ scores in children. The same review said there was not enough evidence to determine whether the 0.7 level used in U.S. water systems has any effect. That report gets quoted a lot, often without the part about the dose.

The American Dental Association and the American Academy of Pediatrics continue to recommend fluoride toothpaste for children and adults, and decades of data show fewer cavities in communities with fluoridated water. Our view is simple: use fluoride where it does the most good, which is on the teeth, and avoid swallowing more than you need.

What Is Dental Fluorosis?

Fluorosis happens when a young child swallows too much fluoride while the adult teeth are still forming under the gums, mostly before age 8. It usually shows up as faint white lines or flecks on the enamel. Mild fluorosis is cosmetic and does not make teeth weaker. Severe fluorosis, with brown staining or pitting, is rare and is usually linked to water with naturally high fluoride.

Not every white spot is fluorosis. Spots can also come from early decay or from braces that were hard to clean around. Our guide to white spots on teeth walks through the differences.

Using Fluoride Wisely at Home

  • Under age 3: a smear of fluoride toothpaste the size of a grain of rice, starting with the first tooth.
  • Ages 3 to 6: a pea sized amount. Brush for your child or with them, and teach them to spit.
  • Older kids and adults: brush twice a day with a fluoride toothpaste and do not rinse heavily right after. Spitting out the extra leaves a protective layer behind.
  • Store toothpaste out of reach. Flavored toothpaste can look like a treat to a toddler.
  • Skip extra products unless recommended. Fluoride rinses and supplements make sense for some people, not everyone.

Our post on how much toothpaste kids should use breaks down the right amounts by age, and how to choose a toothpaste covers what to look for on the label.

Know What Is in Your Water

Many families around Jefferson County use private wells, and well water fluoride varies a lot from one property to the next. Some wells have almost none, and a few have more than is ideal for young children. A simple lab test tells you where you stand, and the answer changes our recommendations for your kids. Read more in our article on well water and fluoride.

If you are on city water, your utility publishes an annual water quality report that lists the fluoride level. If you drink mostly bottled water, check the label, since most bottled water contains little fluoride.

Who Benefits From Extra Fluoride?

Some people are at higher risk for cavities and get real value from a professional fluoride treatment at their checkup. That includes children with past cavities, adults with dry mouth from medications, people with receding gums and exposed roots, and anyone with braces, crowns, or a history of recurring cavities. Professional varnish is painted on in seconds, and very little is swallowed. Adults can learn more in our post on fluoride varnish for adults.

If you would rather avoid fluoride, tell us. We will not lecture you. We will talk through your family's cavity risk, discuss other options such as sealants and diet changes, and make a plan you are comfortable with.

Common Questions

Is fluoride in drinking water safe?

At the level recommended in the United States, 0.7 milligrams per liter, major health organizations consider community water fluoridation safe and effective at reducing cavities. Concerns in recent research involve levels at least twice that amount.

Should my child use fluoride toothpaste?

Yes, in the right amount. Use a smear the size of a grain of rice from the first tooth until age 3, then a pea sized amount from age 3 to 6. Help your child spit rather than swallow.

What is dental fluorosis?

Fluorosis is a change in the look of tooth enamel, usually faint white lines or flecks, caused by swallowing too much fluoride while teeth are forming in early childhood. Mild fluorosis is cosmetic and does not weaken the teeth.
