Tooth Resorption: Signs, Causes, and Treatment

Tooth resorption can quietly damage a tooth from the inside or outside, which makes early diagnosis especially important.

Published August 28, 2026  |  Elite Dental

Tooth resorption is a condition in which the body begins breaking down part of a tooth. This process is normal when a child loses a baby tooth, but it is not normal in a permanent adult tooth. Because resorption can begin without obvious pain, it is sometimes discovered during a routine dental exam or on an X-ray taken for another reason.

The condition can progress slowly or cause significant damage in a relatively short time. The outlook depends on where it starts, how much tooth structure is involved, and how early it is found. A dark spot or unusual X-ray does not automatically mean a tooth must be removed. It does mean the tooth deserves a careful evaluation.

Internal and External Resorption

Internal resorption begins inside the tooth, usually in the space that contains the dental pulp. As it progresses, it can enlarge that inner space and thin the surrounding tooth structure. In some cases, the tooth develops a pink or reddish area because inflamed tissue can be seen through the enamel and dentin.

External resorption starts on the outside surface of the root. It may occur near the gumline, farther down the root, or near the root tip. External cervical resorption, which begins close to the neck of the tooth, can sometimes look like a cavity. The two problems require different treatment, so an accurate diagnosis matters.

Why Does Tooth Resorption Happen?

The exact cause is not always clear. Resorption may be associated with a past injury, orthodontic tooth movement, chronic inflammation, infection, impacted teeth, internal bleaching, or previous dental treatment. A tooth that was bumped years ago can develop a problem long after the injury has been forgotten.

External root resorption can also occur when an impacted tooth presses against the root of a neighboring tooth. In other cases, no single cause can be identified. This uncertainty is one reason regular exams and comparison with older X-rays are valuable.

Possible Warning Signs

Many cases have no early symptoms. When symptoms do occur, they may include a pink spot, a small defect near the gumline, sensitivity, pain, swelling, tooth discoloration, or a tooth that begins to feel loose. These signs can also come from more common dental problems, so an exam is the only reliable way to tell.

How Dentists Diagnose Resorption

Diagnosis begins with a visual examination and dental X-rays. The dentist may test how the tooth responds to temperature, tapping, or pressure and will check the surrounding gums. X-rays taken from different angles can help show whether the damage is inside or outside the tooth.

In complex cases, a three-dimensional CBCT scan may provide a clearer view of the location and extent of the defect. This detail helps determine whether the tooth can be predictably treated and which approach has the best chance of preserving it.

Can a Tooth With Resorption Be Saved?

Many affected teeth can be treated, particularly when the condition is found early. Internal resorption is often managed with root canal treatment to remove the inflamed tissue and stop the process. A crown may be recommended if substantial tooth structure has been weakened.

External resorption treatment depends heavily on its location. The dentist may expose the area, remove the abnormal tissue, and restore the defect. Root canal treatment may also be needed if the pulp is involved. Some small, stable areas can be monitored. If too much root or tooth structure has been lost, extraction and replacement may be the safest long-term option.

What Happens After Treatment?

Follow-up is an important part of care because treatment repairs existing damage but the tooth still needs to be watched. Your dentist may recommend periodic exams and new X-rays to confirm that the resorption has stopped and that the tooth remains stable. The schedule depends on the type of resorption, the treatment performed, and the amount of healthy tooth structure left.

Keep the area clean with gentle brushing and daily cleaning between the teeth. Avoid using the treated tooth to crack ice, hard candy, or other very hard foods. If the tooth develops new sensitivity, swelling, movement, or a change in color, call rather than waiting for the next routine visit. Prompt follow-up can identify a change before it becomes a larger problem.

Do Not Wait for Pain

Pain is not a dependable measure of how serious resorption has become. A tooth may feel normal even while the defect grows. If an X-ray shows a suspicious area, if you notice a pink spot, or if a previously injured tooth changes color, schedule an evaluation promptly.
